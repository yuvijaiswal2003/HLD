Design multi-region active-active for a social app

A social app with 200M daily active users in North America, Europe and Asia currently runs in one AWS region. Users post updates, follow others, like and comment, and read a feed. Make it active-active across three regions so users get low latency and the app survives the loss of an entire region.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • Which operations must be strongly consistent (usernames, account settings) and which can be eventually consistent (likes, feeds)?
  • What are the latency targets for reads and writes, for example p99 under 200 ms for feed reads?
  • What RPO and RTO do we need for a regional loss? Is losing a few seconds of likes acceptable?
  • Are there data residency rules, such as EU users' data staying in the EU?
  • Do most interactions happen between users in the same region, or is the graph global?
  • Is there a budget limit? Active-active means a full data copy in every region plus spare compute in each to absorb a failed region.

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Start by routing: how does a user reach the nearest healthy region, and how is traffic moved when a region fails?
H2Not all data is the same. Split it into data that can be written anywhere and merged, and data that needs a single owner.
H3For single-owner data, consider giving each user a home region that owns their writes. What happens when a user travels or their home region fails?
H4For mergeable data such as like counts and follower sets, which conflict resolution strategies avoid lost updates?
H5Capacity: if one of three regions fails, the remaining two must absorb its traffic. What utilization does that imply for each region at peak?

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

Requirements

  • Functional: post, follow, like, comment, read home feed and profiles; global unique usernames.
  • Latency: reads and writes served in the user's nearest region, p99 under 200 ms.
  • Availability: survive a full regional outage with RTO under 5 minutes for all users.
  • RPO: a few seconds of social interactions may be lost on a regional disaster; account and identity data must not be lost or forked.
  • Consistency: read-your-writes for a user's own actions; eventual consistency across regions for everything else.

Estimates

  • 200M DAU × 50 feed reads a day = 10B reads a day → 10,000,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 115k reads per second, peak ~2x ≈ 230k.
  • Writes: 200M × 10 actions (likes, comments, posts) = 2B a day ≈ 23k writes per second, peak ~50k.
  • Cross-region replication traffic: 50k writes/s × ~1 KB ≈ 50 MB/s to each other region, about 400 Mbps, which is fine for inter-region links but costs real money in transfer fees.
  • Capacity: with 3 regions, losing one sends its share to the other two, so each must handle 1.5x its normal share. Run each region at no more than about 66% of capacity at peak, which is roughly 1.5N in total.

High-level design

Read path: latency-based DNS or anycast sends each user to the nearest healthy region. Feed, posts and profiles are read from that region's local replicas and Redis feed cache. Write path: a like or follow is written locally and acknowledged, then replicated asynchronously to the other two regions (about 100–200 ms of lag). Posts are written in the author's home region. Account changes and username claims go to the strongly consistent global store, which pays a cross-region consensus round trip (100+ ms) but is a small fraction of traffic.

Deep dive: home regions and conflict avoidance

The cleanest way to avoid write conflicts is to have one owner per record. Each user gets a home region (usually where they signed up), stored in a small global directory and cached at the edge. Writes to that user's own data (profile, posts) are routed to the home region; everyone reads anywhere. When a user travels, their reads stay local and only their own-data writes pay a cross-region hop. If the home region fails, ownership moves to a surviving region; to avoid two owners, ownership changes go through the consensus-backed directory with a fencing epoch, and writes carrying an old epoch are rejected.

Deep dive: mergeable data

  • Counters: never replicate "likes = 1,042"; replicate increments. Each region keeps its own count per post, and the displayed total is the sum. Two regions incrementing concurrently never lose updates (a G-counter, or a PN-counter for unlikes).
  • Sets such as followers: use an add-wins set or record each edge with a timestamp and tombstones for removal, so a concurrent follow and unfollow resolves deterministically.
  • Idempotent events: every replicated event carries a unique ID; consumers dedupe, so redelivery during failover does not double-count.
  • Read-your-writes: the user's own region shows their action immediately. If a failover moves them to another region before replication arrives, the client keeps recent actions locally and merges them into what it shows for a short window.

Deep dive: regional failover

  1. Detect
    Health checks from multiple vantage points fail for the region for about 30–60 seconds. Use automated detection but require a quorum of checkers to avoid false evacuations.
  2. Evacuate
    Remove the region from DNS or anycast. Clients move to the next-nearest region; DNS TTLs of 30–60 seconds limit how long stragglers keep trying the dead one.
  3. Absorb
    The two survivors take 1.5x load. Because they run at 66% or less at peak, they cope without needing to launch capacity, which may be slow during a large incident. Non-critical features (trending, recommendations) are shed if needed.
  4. Reassign ownership
    Users homed in the failed region get a new home via the directory, with a new epoch. Writes from the failed region that never replicated, a few seconds' worth, are lost or recovered when it returns.
  5. Recover
    When the region returns, it catches up from the others' replication logs before taking traffic, and ownership is moved back gradually.

5Follow-up questions

  • EU regulation now requires EU users' personal data to stay in the EU. How does that change replication and the home-region model?
  • A network partition splits us-east from the other two regions, but us-east is still healthy and serving its users. What should happen to writes there?
  • How would you test that a regional evacuation really works without causing an outage?
  • A celebrity's post gets 5M likes in an hour across all regions. How do counters and replication hold up?
  • How do you run a schema migration across three active regions without downtime?
