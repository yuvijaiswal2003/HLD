Design a payment service with 99.99% availability

Design the payment service for an e-commerce platform. Checkout calls it to charge a customer's card through external payment processors such as Stripe or Adyen. It must be available 99.99% of the time, must never charge a customer twice, and must never lose a record of a successful charge.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • Do we process cards ourselves or call external processors (PSPs)? How many PSPs, and are they interchangeable?
  • What is the peak payment volume, for example during a holiday sale?
  • Is 99.99% measured on our API's success rate, or does it include the processors' outages?
  • What latency does checkout expect, and can a payment be confirmed asynchronously ("processing") in some cases?
  • Which regions do we operate in, and are there data residency or PCI DSS scope constraints?
  • What is the acceptable data loss (RPO) for the payment ledger? Is it zero?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Start with the budget: 99.99% is about 4.4 minutes of downtime a month. Which components are in series on the payment path, and what availability does each need?
H2Retries are unavoidable with networks and external processors. What makes a retried charge safe?
H3Treat the ledger as the source of truth. Which state must be written durably before and after calling the processor, and what happens if the process crashes between the two?
H4The processor itself might be down or slow. How do you avoid letting that consume all your threads, and can you route to a second processor?
H5Think about RPO zero: what replication setup gives no data loss on an AZ failure, and what do you do about a full regional loss?

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

Requirements

  • Functional: create a payment for an order, capture or refund it, query status, and receive asynchronous results from processors via webhooks.
  • Exactly-once effect: a payment for an order is charged at most once regardless of client, service or processor retries.
  • Availability: 99.99% success for valid requests at our API, about 4.4 minutes of failure budget a month.
  • Durability: RPO zero for the payment ledger within a region; no successful charge may be unrecorded.
  • Latency: p99 under 2 seconds including the processor call, which dominates.
  • Security: never store raw card numbers; use processor tokens to keep PCI scope small.

Estimates

  • 10M payments a day → 10,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 115 per second on average; assume 10x at peak sales, about 1,200 per second.
  • Each payment writes about 3–5 ledger rows (created, authorized, captured, plus state transitions) at ~500 bytes: 10M × 5 × 500 B ≈ 25 GB a day, about 9 TB a year. A sharded or large relational cluster handles this.
  • Processor calls take 300–800 ms. In-flight calls at peak, by Little's Law: 1,200 × 0.8 s ≈ ~1,000 concurrent, so size connection pools and threads accordingly.
  • Availability budget: if our service needs 99.99% end to end and has about four series dependencies (LB, app, ledger DB, processor), each must be about 99.9975% or better (0.999975^4 ≈ 0.9999), which a single instance of anything rarely is. So every tier must be redundant, and the processor dependency must be made redundant or taken off the synchronous critical path.

Data model

A relational database (PostgreSQL or Aurora/Spanner-class) because payments need transactions, unique constraints and strong consistency. Run it multi-AZ with synchronous replication for RPO zero. Shard by order_id hash if one primary cannot take the write rate.

High-level design

Write path: checkout sends POST /payments with an idempotency key. The service inserts the key and a PENDING payment row in one transaction (a unique-key conflict means it is a retry: return the stored result). It then calls the processor, passing our payment_id as the processor's own idempotency key, so even our retries to the processor cannot double-charge. On a response it updates the row to SUCCEEDED or FAILED and writes an outbox row in the same transaction; a relay publishes it to Kafka for order fulfillment. Read path: status reads go to the primary (or a standby run with synchronous_commit = remote_apply, so it has applied every acknowledged write), never a lagging replica.

Deep dive: idempotency and the unknown outcome

The dangerous case is a timeout from the processor: the charge may or may not have happened. Mark the payment UNKNOWN, return 202 PROCESSING to checkout, and resolve it later. Because the processor call carried our idempotency key, retrying the same request is safe and returns the original outcome; the processor's webhook or a status query settles it. A reconciliation job compares our ledger with the processor's settlement reports daily (and status APIs every few minutes for UNKNOWN rows) and fixes any drift. Never retry an unknown payment with a new key.

Deep dive: surviving processor failures

  • A circuit breaker per processor with a 2-second timeout: if processor A's error rate passes the threshold, the breaker opens and new payments route to processor B. Card tokens must work with both, for example using a network token or a vault that can forward to either.
  • A bulkhead per processor: separate connection pools so a hanging processor cannot exhaust capacity for the healthy one.
  • If every processor is down, accept the payment as PROCESSING into a durable queue and retry with backoff and jitter. This counts as success against our availability SLO when the business accepts delayed confirmation for low-risk orders.
  • Retry only at the payment service layer; checkout does not retry on top of it, which avoids retry amplification.

Deep dive: database failover and regional DR

Within the region, the primary has a synchronous standby in another AZ (RPO zero). Failover is automated: detect after ~10–15 seconds of failed checks, fence the old primary, promote, update the endpoint; total around 30–60 seconds, which uses a large part of the monthly 4.4-minute budget, so it must be rare and rehearsed. For a regional disaster, run a warm standby region with an asynchronously replicated database: RPO of a few seconds of payments, recovered from processor records by reconciliation, and RTO around 10–15 minutes.

5Follow-up questions

  • How do you deploy a new version without spending the error budget? Walk through canary releases and automatic rollback.
  • The synchronous standby becomes unreachable. Do you block writes or degrade to asynchronous replication, and what does each do to RPO and availability?
  • A webhook from the processor arrives before your own call returns. How do you handle out-of-order updates to the payment state machine?
  • Finance reports a mismatch between your ledger and the processor's settlement file. How does reconciliation find and fix it?
  • What would change if the business required active-active across two regions for payments?
