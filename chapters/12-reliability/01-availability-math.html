Availability math and redundancy

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) a system serves correctly. For a single component it is A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR): mean time between failures divided by total time. A server that fails on average every 1,000 hours and takes 1 hour to replace is up 1000 / 1001 ≈ 99.9% of the time. Notice the lever this gives you: cutting MTTR from 1 hour to 6 minutes buys as much as making the hardware fail ten times less often. Fast detection, automated failover and quick rollback are availability features.

Chapter 3 has the full table of nines. This lesson is about the next step: computing the availability of a system from the availability of its parts, and finding the parts that cap it.

Components in series

If a request needs every component to work (load balancer, then app server, then database), the components are in series and their availabilities multiply: A = A1 × A2 × ... × An. Two 99.9% components give 0.999 × 0.999 = 0.998001, about 99.8%. Downtime roughly doubles, from 8.8 to about 17.5 hours a year. Ten 99.9% dependencies in series give 0.999^10 ≈ 0.990, which is 99.0% or about 3.6 days a year. Every synchronous dependency you add lowers the ceiling.

Components in parallel

If any one of several redundant copies can serve the request, they are in parallel, and you multiply the unavailabilities: A = 1 − (1 − A1) × (1 − A2). Two independent 99.9% replicas give 1 − (0.001)^2 = 1 − 0.000001 = 0.999999, six nines, roughly 32 seconds of downtime a year. Three 99% servers give 1 − 0.01^3 = 99.9999%. Redundancy is how you build a reliable system out of unreliable parts.

A worked example. Take a three-tier stack and compute it the way you would on a whiteboard, tier by tier (parallel inside a tier, series across tiers):

Design B shows the key insight: once the app and DB tiers are redundant, the single load balancer dominates the result. The end-to-end number is set by the weakest series component, so you find that one and fix it next. That weakest link is a single point of failure (SPOF): one component whose failure takes the whole system down.

Pitfall
The parallel formula is optimistic
It assumes failures are independent and failover is instant. Neither is true. Both replicas run the same bad deploy, share the same expired certificate, sit in the same AZ when its power fails, or read the same broken config. Failover takes 10–60 seconds to detect and execute, and the failover machinery itself can fail. Design C will not deliver one minute a year. Correlated failures are why you spread replicas across AZs, stagger deploys and keep dependencies diverse.

Hidden single points of failure

  • A single database primary, cache cluster, message broker or load balancer.
  • Shared dependencies everyone forgets: DNS provider, auth service, config/feature-flag service, secrets store, container registry.
  • A single region, or one AZ that holds all the replicas of a shard.
  • Operational SPOFs: an unrenewed TLS certificate, a cron job on one box, the one engineer who knows how to fail over.

Redundancy levels: N, N+1, N+2, 2N

LevelMeaningSurvivesTypical use
NExactly enough capacity for peak loadNothing; any failure degrades serviceDev and test
N+1One spare unitOne failure, or one unit down for maintenanceMost stateless tiers
N+2Two sparesA failure during a deploy or maintenance windowCritical tiers, quorum systems
2NA full duplicate of everythingLoss of an entire siteActive-active data centers or regions

Apply this to zones. If peak needs N = 6 servers and you spread across 3 AZs, losing one AZ leaves two, which must still hold 6, so each AZ needs 3 and you run 9 total (1.5N). With only 2 AZs each needs all 6: 12 total (2N). In general, with k zones you provision N × k / (k − 1). More zones means less idle headroom.

In the interview
How to talk about availability
State the target in minutes ("99.99% is about 4.4 minutes a month"), multiply the series path, point at the SPOFs, add redundancy where it moves the number, and size capacity so a zone loss at peak still leaves N. Then say the honest part: real availability is limited by correlated failures and MTTR, so you also invest in fast detection, automated failover and safe deploys.
