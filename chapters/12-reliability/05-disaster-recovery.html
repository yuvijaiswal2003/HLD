Disaster recovery, backups and chaos engineering

High availability handles a node or an AZ failing. Disaster recovery (DR) handles the bigger events: losing a region, losing a database, or losing the data itself to corruption, an accidental delete, a bad migration or ransomware. Two numbers frame every DR conversation.

RPO and RTO

RPO (recovery point objective) is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time: an RPO of 5 minutes means after recovery you may be missing up to the last 5 minutes of writes. RTO (recovery time objective) is how long you can afford to be down: from the disaster until service is restored. RPO is driven by how often you copy data off-site (backup frequency or replication lag); RTO is driven by how fast you can detect, decide, restore and redirect.

Backups done properly

  • Full, incremental and differential backups trade restore speed against storage. Disk snapshots are fast and cheap to take.
  • Point-in-time recovery (PITR): a base backup plus the archived write-ahead log (PostgreSQL WAL, MySQL binlog) lets you restore to any second, for example just before a bad DELETE.
  • The 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies, on 2 different media or services, 1 off-site (another region or account).
  • Make backups immutable (for example S3 Object Lock) and keep them in a separate account so an attacker or a buggy script with production credentials cannot delete them.
  • Test restores regularly and time them. Restoring 1 TB at 200 MB/s takes about 5,000 seconds, roughly 1.4 hours, before any replay or validation. That is your RTO floor.
Pitfall
Replication is not a backup
Replicas copy every change, including the mistakes. A DROP TABLE or a corrupting bug reaches every synchronous replica within milliseconds. Replication protects against hardware loss; only backups with history (snapshots, PITR) protect against bad writes.
In production
GitLab, January 2017
While fixing replication, an engineer deleted the data directory on GitLab.com's primary database. Several of the backup and replication mechanisms they relied on turned out not to be working, and the site was restored from a copy taken about six hours earlier, losing roughly six hours of data. GitLab published the postmortem openly; the lesson everyone took from it was that an untested backup is not a backup.

Pick per system, not per company. Compare the cost of an hour of downtime with the cost of the DR setup. A payments database may justify warm standby or active-active; an internal analytics warehouse is fine with backup and restore. Many companies tier their services (tier 0 for revenue-critical, tier 3 for internal tools) and assign each tier an RPO, RTO and strategy.

Chaos engineering

The only way to know your failover, retries and fallbacks work is to break things on purpose. Chaos engineering runs controlled experiments that inject failures into a system (ideally production) to find weaknesses before they cause outages. Tools include Netflix's Chaos Monkey, Gremlin, LitmusChaos and AWS Fault Injection Service.

  1. Define steady state
    Pick a measurable signal of normal behavior, such as successful checkouts per minute or the SLO error rate.
  2. Form a hypothesis
    "If we terminate one database replica, read error rate stays under 0.1% and p99 stays under 300 ms."
  3. Inject a real-world failure
    Kill instances, add 500 ms of latency, blackhole a dependency, fill a disk, or fail an entire AZ.
  4. Limit the blast radius
    Start in staging, then a small percentage of production traffic, with an abort button that stops the experiment automatically if the steady state breaks.
  5. Fix and automate
    Every surprise becomes a fix. Experiments that pass become recurring automated checks so regressions are caught.
In production
Netflix
Netflix built Chaos Monkey during its move to AWS to randomly terminate production instances during business hours, forcing every service to tolerate instance loss. Its later Chaos Kong exercises simulate losing an entire AWS region by evacuating traffic to the other regions Netflix runs in active-active.
In the interview
Close the loop
When you present a design, give its RPO and RTO explicitly ("RPO zero inside the region from sync replication, about 1 second for a regional loss; RTO about 15 minutes with warm standby"), say how backups are protected and tested, and mention game days. It shows you think about running the system, not just drawing it.
