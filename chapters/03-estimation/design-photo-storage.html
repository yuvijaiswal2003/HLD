Estimate and design storage for a photo-sharing app

Design the photo storage and delivery layer for a photo-sharing app with 100 million daily active users. Users upload photos from their phones; other users see them in feeds and on profile pages at several sizes. Photos are kept until the user deletes them. Estimate storage, bandwidth and cost over five years, and design the upload, storage and serving paths. Feed ranking is out of scope.

45:00Mock interview timer. Start it when you begin talking.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • How many photos are uploaded per day, and how many photos does a user view per day?
  • What is the maximum upload size? Do we keep the original, and which display sizes do clients need?
  • Are videos in scope, or only photos?
  • How fast must a new photo be visible to followers after upload?
  • Where are users located, and what load-time target do we have for images?
  • What durability is expected? Is losing any photo acceptable?
  • How must deletion work, for example for privacy regulations?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Start with uploads per day × bytes per photo, and remember that each photo is stored at several sizes.
H2Compare the size of the photo bytes with the size of their metadata. Should they live in the same store?
H3Compute egress for viewing at peak. Could your own servers send that? What changes with a CDN?
H4Most views go to recent photos. What does that suggest for photos that are a year old?
H5Does an upload need to pass through your API servers at all, or can the phone send bytes straight to object storage?

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

Requirements

  • Functional: upload a photo from a phone (up to 20 MB, JPEG or HEIC); serve each photo at four sizes (150, 320, 640 and 1080 px wide); delete a photo; photos are kept until deleted.
  • Non-functional: 100M DAU worldwide; a new photo is viewable within ~5 s of upload; image load p95 under 200 ms; 99.95% availability for viewing; no photo is ever lost (target 11 nines of durability); cost-efficient over five years.

Estimates

text
Uploads
  100M DAU x 0.2 uploads/day           = 20M photos/day
  20M / 10^5 s                         = ~200 uploads/s avg, ~600/s peak

Bytes per photo
  original, resized on the phone to 2048 px   ~1.5 MB
  derived sizes: 1080 px ~250 KB, 640 px ~100 KB,
                 320 px ~30 KB, 150 px ~10 KB  ~0.4 MB
  total                                       ~1.9 MB, call it 2 MB

Photo storage (logical)
  20M x 2 MB                           = 40 TB/day
  40 TB x 365                          = ~14.6 PB/year
  x 5 years                            = ~73 PB

Metadata
  20M rows/day x 500 B                 = 10 GB/day = ~3.7 TB/year, ~18 TB in 5 years

Upload ingress at peak
  600/s x 1.5 MB                       = 900 MB/s = ~7.2 Gbit/s

Views
  100M DAU x 100 views/day             = 10B views/day
  10^10 / 10^5 s                       = 100k views/s avg, ~300k/s peak
  ~150 KB per view (mostly 640 and 1080 px)
  peak egress: 300k x 150 KB           = 45 GB/s = ~360 Gbit/s
  origin share at 95% CDN hit ratio    = ~18 Gbit/s, ~15k requests/s

Three conclusions drive the design. Photo bytes (73 PB) dwarf metadata (18 TB) by 4,000x, so bytes go to object storage and metadata to a database. Peak egress of 360 Gbit/s requires a CDN. And storage grows by 14.6 PB a year forever, so the cost per stored byte is what we must optimize.

High-level design

Upload path: the app asks the API for an upload slot; the API creates a metadata row with status uploading and returns a short-lived presigned URL. The phone PUTs the bytes directly to object storage, so 7 Gbit/s of upload traffic never touches our servers. On complete, the API verifies the object exists, sets status to processing and enqueues a job. A resize worker reads the original, writes the four sizes and marks the photo ready, all within a few seconds. View path: the feed returns CDN URLs; the CDN serves about 95% of bytes from its edge caches and fetches misses from object storage.

Deep dives

Storage tiering is where the money is. After year one we hold 14.6 PB. At roughly $0.021 per GB-month (approximate S3 Standard list price at high volume), that is about 14.6M GB × $0.021 ≈ $307,000 per month, and growing. But the original is 1.5 of the 1.9 MB per photo, and is rarely read after resizing. Keep the derived sizes (21% of bytes, 3.1 PB) in standard storage: about $65,000. Move originals (11.5 PB) to an archive tier with millisecond access such as S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval at about $0.004 per GB-month: about $46,000. Total ≈ $110,000 per month, roughly 64% less. A lifecycle rule can also move derived sizes of photos older than a year to an infrequent-access tier.

Resize capacity. Assume ~200 ms of CPU to decode a 2048 px image and encode four sizes. Peak is 600 uploads/s × 0.2 s = 120 cores busy; at a 60% utilization target that is 200 cores, about 7 servers with 32 cores. The queue absorbs bursts: on New Year's Eve, at 10x uploads, workers fall behind and photos take longer to become ready instead of failing, and autoscaling adds workers based on queue depth. Generating sizes eagerly costs storage for sizes nobody requests, but avoids slow first views; on-demand resizing is the alternative (Chapter 5 designs an image resizing CDN).

Durability and origin load. Managed object storage replicates or erasure-codes data across zones for us. If we ran our own storage, 3x replication of 73 PB would need 219 PB of raw disk, while a 10+4 erasure code needs 1.4x, about 102 PB (Chapter 10 explains the trade-off). At the origin, 15k GET/s at peak exceeds S3's documented baseline of 5,500 GETs per second per prefix, which is why object keys start with a hash prefix that spreads requests across many partitions.

5Follow-up questions

  • Users can now upload 60-second videos. Redo the storage and egress estimates.
  • Many users repost identical memes. How would you detect duplicates, and what would it save?
  • A user deletes a photo for privacy reasons. How do you make sure it disappears from every size, every storage tier and every CDN cache?
  • 30% of users are on slow mobile networks. What would you change about the image sizes and formats you serve?
  • Uploads spike 10x for an hour on New Year's Eve. How many resize workers do you need, and what happens if you don't scale?
