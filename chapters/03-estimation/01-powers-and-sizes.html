Powers of two, data sizes and the size of things

Back-of-the-envelope estimation means working out rough numbers (requests per second, storage, bandwidth, memory, machine count) fast enough to do on a whiteboard. The goal is not precision. The goal is the right order of magnitude, because that is what decides the design: 50 writes per second fits on any database, 50,000 needs careful choices, and 5 million needs partitioning. Being off by 2x rarely changes the design; being off by 100x always does.

Arithmetic rules for the whiteboard

  • Round to one or two significant figures. 86,400 becomes 100,000; 365 becomes 400 when it makes the math easy (and say you rounded up).
  • Work in powers of ten. 3 × 10^6 × 2 × 10^3 = 6 × 10^9. Add exponents when multiplying, subtract when dividing.
  • Carry units everywhere. “200 M posts/day × 1 KB/post = 200 GB/day.” Units catch mistakes like mixing bits and bytes.
  • State assumptions out loud and write them down, so the interviewer can correct an input instead of doubting the whole result.
  • Say what the number means. An estimate is only useful when you finish with “...so this fits on one node” or “...so we need a CDN”.

Powers of two and data units

Computers count in powers of two, and 2^10 = 1,024 is close enough to 1,000 that each named unit is about a thousand times the previous one. Memorize this ladder and you can convert any size instantly.

Power of twoExact valueApproximatelyUnit
2^101,02410^3, a thousand1 KB (kilobyte)
2^201,048,57610^6, a million1 MB (megabyte)
2^301,073,741,82410^9, a billion1 GB (gigabyte)
2^40~1.1 × 10^1210^12, a trillion1 TB (terabyte)
2^50~1.13 × 10^1510^15, a quadrillion1 PB (petabyte)

Strictly, 1 KB is 1,000 bytes and 1 KiB (kibibyte) is 1,024 bytes. Disk vendors use decimal units, operating systems often show binary ones, which is why a “1 TB” disk shows up as about 931 GiB. The gap is 2.4% at KB and about 10% at TB. In interviews, ignore it.

In production
The view counter that hit 2^31
In December 2014, Psy's “Gangnam Style” passed 2,147,483,647 views on YouTube, the maximum value of a signed 32-bit integer. YouTube said it had already moved the counter to a 64-bit integer. Any ID or counter that can plausibly pass 2 billion should be 64-bit from day one.

When an object isn't in the table, build its size from its fields. Here is a post row:

text
post_id                   8 B   (64-bit ID)
author_id                 8 B
created_at                8 B
text                   ~200 B   (avg ~140 chars, some multi-byte)
language, flags         ~10 B
like/reply/share counts  24 B   (3 x 8 B)
media ids               ~16 B
------------------------------
raw row                ~275 B
x ~2 for row headers, indexes, free space
= ~550 B   -> round up to 1 KB per post
Tip
Round object sizes up
Real storage is bigger than the sum of the fields: row headers, indexes, page fill factors, replication metadata. Rounding a 275-byte row up to 1 KB is not sloppy; it is roughly right, and it makes every later multiplication easy.
