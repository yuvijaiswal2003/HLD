Design a News-Search Indexer for a Crawler's Output

The web crawler from this chapter publishes a page-crawled event for every fetch from about 50,000 news sites. Design the system that turns this stream into news search: articles become searchable within about a minute of being crawled, results are ranked by relevance and recency, syndicated copies of one story are grouped together, and users can filter by date, source and language. Assume 2M new articles per day and a five-year archive.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • What is the freshness target from crawl to searchable: seconds, a minute, an hour?
  • How much history must be searchable, and must old articles be as fast as recent ones?
  • How should a wire story republished by hundreds of sites appear: once, or every copy?
  • What happens when an article is corrected after indexing, or must be taken down?
  • What query volume, and which sorts and filters: relevance, newest first, date ranges, source?
  • Which languages must be supported?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Treat the search index as a derived view. Keep extracted articles in object storage so the search cluster can be rebuilt or reindexed at any time.
H2An article is crawled many times and events can arrive out of order. Which document ID and which versioning rule make indexing idempotent and order-safe?
H3News search is mostly about the last few days. How would you lay out indices by time so a query touches few shards and old data sits on cheaper hardware?
H4Syndicated copies differ only in boilerplate. SimHash plus a pigeonhole argument finds near-duplicates among millions of articles without comparing every pair.
H5BM25 alone ranks a three-year-old article above today's news. Blend text relevance with recency decay and source authority.

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: extract article text and metadata from crawled pages; index new and updated articles; keyword search with filters (date range, source, language) and sorts (relevance, newest); group syndicated copies; honor corrections and takedowns.
  • Non-functional: crawl-to-searchable p95 under 60 s; search p99 under 300 ms for the last 30 days and under 2 s for the archive; indexing is idempotent and survives replays; the index can be rebuilt from durable storage.

2. Estimates

Input: ~20M news page fetches/day (front pages, sitemaps and recrawls) ≈ 230 events/s, ~10x during breaking news. About 2M are new articles and 1M are changed ones, so ~3M index writes/day ≈ 35/s, ~350/s at peak, which is light work for an Elasticsearch cluster. At ~20 ms of CPU to parse and extract a page, 230/s needs ~5 cores on average and ~50 at peak, so an autoscaled pool of extractor workers. Storage: ~5 KB of text plus ~1 KB of metadata per article, ~8 KB once indexed: 2M × 8 KB = 16 GB/day, and five years of 3.65B articles is ~29 TB of primaries, ~58 TB with one replica. The last 30 days are only ~480 GB of primaries. Queries: 10M/day ≈ 115/s, up to ~1,000/s during big events, with ~90% filtered to the last week.

4. Data model

FieldType in the indexPurpose
_id = URL fingerprintkeywordOne document per URL, so recrawls overwrite instead of duplicating
title, bodytext, per-language analyzerFull-text matching; title boosted
published_at, first_seen_atdateFilters, sorting, recency decay, index routing
source, langkeywordFilters and source authority lookup
cluster_idkeywordGroups syndicated copies; used for field collapsing
simhash64-bit integerNear-duplicate detection
authorityfloatPer-source quality score used in ranking

Elasticsearch (or OpenSearch) serves search, but it is not the source of truth (Chapter 10): every extracted article is also written as compressed JSON lines to an S3 article store, partitioned by day. Reindexing for a new analyzer or mapping is then a batch job from S3 into new indices followed by an alias switch, with no recrawl. SimHash lookup tables for the last week live in Redis.

5. High-level design

  1. Consume
    Extractor workers in one consumer group read page-crawled, skip non-200 responses and pages whose content_fp is unchanged since the last indexed version.
  2. Extract
    Fetch the WARC record from S3, strip navigation, ads and comments, and keep title, body, author and candidate publish dates.
  3. Cluster
    Compute the body's SimHash, look up near-duplicates from the last 7 days, and join their cluster or start a new one.
  4. Enrich and persist
    Detect the language, pick published_at, attach the source's authority score, and write the article to the S3 store.
  5. Index
    Send bulk requests of a few MB to the daily index chosen by first_seen_at; commit Kafka offsets only after the bulk call succeeds.

6. Deep dives

A. Idempotent, order-safe indexing. The document ID is the URL fingerprint, and the index is chosen by the article's first_seen_at date, so every recrawl of a URL targets the same document in the same index. Each write carries version = fetched_at in milliseconds with version_type=external; Elasticsearch then accepts a write only if its version is higher than the stored one. A late, older crawl is rejected with a version conflict instead of overwriting newer content, and a Kafka replay after a worker crash is harmless. This is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent writes (Chapter 9). With the default 1-second refresh on hot indices, a document is searchable about a second after its bulk request, so end-to-end latency is dominated by crawl-to-Kafka and extraction, well under the 60-second target.

B. Grouping syndicated stories. Two copies count as near-duplicates if their 64-bit SimHashes differ in at most 3 bits. Split each fingerprint into 4 blocks of 16 bits; by the pigeonhole principle, if at most 3 bits differ, at least one block is identical. Keep 4 lookup tables keyed by (block number, block value) covering the last 7 days, about 14M articles, since syndication happens within days. A new article fetches the candidates in its 4 buckets, about 14M / 65,536 ≈ 214 per bucket, so ~850 XOR-and-popcount checks taking microseconds, and joins the cluster of the closest match. At query time, Elasticsearch field collapsing on cluster_id returns the best-ranked copy of each story with a count of the others.

C. Ranking and time-based layout. Relevance sort uses a function_score query: BM25 over title^3 and body, multiplied by a Gaussian decay on published_at (for example, the score halves at about 2 days) and by a function of source authority. Publish dates come from article:published_time meta tags or JSON-LD datePublished, then sitemap news tags, falling back to first_seen_at; dates in the future are clamped, which stops sites from gaming recency. Index layout: one index per day of ~16 GB with one primary shard, inside the common 10–50 GB per-shard guideline. The query service targets only the indices its date filter covers, so a “last 7 days” search touches 7 shards rather than all 1,825 daily indices. After 30 days, index lifecycle management moves an index to cheaper warm nodes and force-merges it; the archive beyond a year can sit on searchable snapshots in object storage.

5Follow-up questions

  • A major event brings 20x normal crawl volume and 50x normal queries at once. What protects freshness and query latency?
  • How would you add entity pages, such as every article about one company?
  • An editor says a correction took 20 minutes to appear in search. Trace where that time could have gone.
  • How would you support Japanese and Chinese, where words are not separated by spaces?
  • How would you personalize ranking without losing the ability to cache popular queries?
