Design ticket booking and flash sales (BookMyShow / Ticketmaster)

Ticket booking is the classic inventory that must not be oversold problem; a flash sale is the same problem with a stampede at the door. A normal day is small, a few dozen bookings per second. The real load arrives in bursts, when two million fans show up at 10:00 for 60,000 seats. Chapter 6 covered the database side of not double-booking. This walkthrough covers the whole system: holding seats while someone pays, charging exactly once, and keeping the stampede from reaching the database at all. The 2022 Taylor Swift Eras Tour presale, where Ticketmaster reported about 3.5 billion system requests, roughly four times its previous peak, shows what happens when the front door gives way.

1. Requirements

  • Browse: events, shows and venues, with a seat map showing near-live availability.
  • Hold: select up to N seats and hold them for a fixed window (say 10 minutes) while paying.
  • Pay and ticket: pay, then receive tickets (QR codes) in the app and by email; refunds follow event policy.
  • High-demand on-sales: a fair queue, per-account purchase limits and bot defenses.
  • Correctness: no seat is sold twice; nobody is charged without getting a ticket (or is refunded automatically); nobody is charged twice.
  • Fairness: queue position decides access, not refresh speed or a bot farm.
  • Availability: browsing stays up even while booking is throttled; the sale must not fall over at 10:00.
  • Latency: holds and confirmations under ~500 ms; queue position updates every few seconds.

2. Estimates

text
Steady state (BookMyShow-like): 1M bookings/day
  1M / 86,400                             ~ 12 bookings/s, ~60/s on a Friday evening
  ~20 seat-map views per booking          ~ 250 reads/s, ~1,200/s at peak
Flash sale: one 60,000-seat stadium, 2M fans online at 10:00
  no queue: 2M fans refreshing every 2 s  = 1M requests/s at the front door
  orders needed: 60,000 seats / 3 per order        = 20,000 orders
  admit 5,000 fans/min; each takes ~3 min to pick seats and pay
    Little's Law: 5,000/min x 3 min       = 15,000 fans active at once
    hold attempts ~5,000/min              ~ 85 writes/s   -> one PostgreSQL primary is plenty
    if ~80% buy: 4,000 orders/min         -> sold out in ~5 minutes
  seat map: 60,000 seats x 2 bits         = 15 KB; 15,000 fans re-fetch every 2 s
                                          ~ 7,500 reads/s x 15 KB ~ 110 MB/s from cache
  the other ~1.98M fans need a fast, cheap "sold out" answer

This arithmetic is the whole argument for the design. The correctness-critical path, holds and confirmations, needs well under a thousand writes per second even during the biggest on-sale, because inventory caps it: only 60,000 seats exist. The danger is the million requests per second at the front door. Scaling the booking tier to match would be expensive and pointless, so instead the waiting room meters admission to what inventory and the booking tier can absorb. Chapter 4's flash-sale problem covered autoscaling the web tier; here the answer is to absorb the spike at the edge and never let it reach the database.

5. High-level design

On a normal day, requests go straight to the booking service. For a high-demand on-sale the event is flagged as queued, and any request for its pages without a valid access token is redirected to the waiting room: a static page served from the CDN that polls a tiny status endpoint. When a fan is admitted, the waiting room issues a signed token (account, event, expiry) that the API gateway checks without a database lookup. The booking service reads seat maps from a Redis snapshot refreshed about every second, takes holds with a conditional update in PostgreSQL, creates an order from the hold, and starts payment. The gateway's webhook confirms the order, which flips the seats from HELD to BOOKED and issues tickets. Change data capture streams every seat change to Kafka, which refreshes the seat-map snapshot, releases expired holds early and sends tickets.

6. Deep dives

Deep dive A: holds with a TTL and optimistic concurrency. A seat is AVAILABLE, HELD (with hold_id and hold_expires_at) or BOOKED. Taking a hold is one UPDATE whose WHERE clause states what must be true: the seat is available, or its previous hold has expired. This is optimistic concurrency. Nothing is locked while the fan studies the seat map, and the write simply fails if someone got there first. The row lock lasts only the milliseconds of the statement. Under PostgreSQL's READ COMMITTED, a concurrent UPDATE on the same row waits for the first to commit, then re-checks the WHERE clause against the new row version and updates nothing. A multi-seat hold is all-or-nothing in one transaction. Two concurrent holds that share two or more seats can deadlock if they lock those rows in different orders, so lock in seat-ID order (a short SELECT ... ORDER BY seat_id FOR UPDATE before the UPDATE does it) and retry the rare deadlock. Expired holds don't need a sweeper for correctness, because the next hold attempt reclaims them. A sweeper, or a delayed message per hold (Chapter 9), just releases them sooner so the seat map is accurate. Version-based optimistic locking (WHERE version = :v) is equivalent and natural when the client sends back the version it saw.

sql
-- Hold A12 and A13 for 10 minutes, all or nothing
BEGIN;
SELECT seat_id FROM seats                -- lock rows in a fixed order: no deadlocks
 WHERE show_id = :show_id AND seat_id = ANY(:seat_ids)
 ORDER BY seat_id FOR UPDATE;            -- held for milliseconds, not think time
UPDATE seats
   SET status = 'HELD', hold_id = :hold_id,
       hold_expires_at = now() + interval '10 minutes',
       version = version + 1
 WHERE show_id = :show_id
   AND seat_id = ANY(:seat_ids)
   AND (status = 'AVAILABLE'
        OR (status = 'HELD' AND hold_expires_at < now()));  -- reclaim expired holds
-- app: if rows updated != 2 then ROLLBACK and report which seat was taken
COMMIT;

-- Confirm after payment: succeeds only if this hold still owns the seats
UPDATE seats SET status = 'BOOKED', version = version + 1
 WHERE show_id = :show_id AND hold_id = :hold_id AND status = 'HELD';
-- 0 rows: someone reclaimed the seats after expiry, so refund automatically

SELECT FOR UPDATE until payment

  • Strict serialization of buyers
Weaknesses

  • Connection pool exhaustion under load

  • Expiry is a column, reclaimed lazily
  • Hundreds to low thousands of writes/s per show is easy
Weaknesses

  • The seat map must be a separate cached snapshot

Redis SET NX EX per seat

Strengths
  • Very fast, around 100k ops/s per node
  • TTL expiry is automatic
  • Multi-seat atomicity with a Lua script
Weaknesses
  • Async replication: a failover can lose holds and double-hold a seat
  • Two stores must be kept in agreement

Deep dive B: paying exactly once. The order is created from the hold under an idempotency key, so a double-click or a client retry returns the same order (Chapter 11). The charge request to the gateway (Stripe, Razorpay, Adyen) passes the order ID as the gateway's own idempotency key, so a retried charge never becomes a second charge. Confirmation is driven by the gateway's signed webhook, which can arrive twice or late, so confirming is itself a conditional update: seats go from HELD to BOOKED only where hold_id matches and the status is still HELD. A duplicate webhook finds nothing to change and still returns 200. The dangerous race is a payment that succeeds after the hold expired and the seats were resold. Prevent most of it by making the payment session expire a minute or two before the hold. Handle the rest by refunding automatically, and idempotently, whenever confirmation updates zero rows. If the hold expired but nobody reclaimed the seats, the confirmation still succeeds, which is the kindest outcome. A daily reconciliation job matches gateway settlements against orders to catch anything a lost webhook missed.

Deep dive C: the virtual waiting room. You can't scale a relational database to a million writes per second in a minute, and you shouldn't try, because only 60,000 seats exist. The waiting room turns an uncontrolled spike into a metered flow. Fans who arrive early sit on a static pre-queue page served by the CDN. At the start time, everyone already waiting gets a random position, so arriving at 9:58 is no worse than 9:59:59 and refreshing gains nothing; later arrivals line up in arrival order. The state is two numbers per event: a counter that hands out queue numbers (Redis INCR) and a now-serving number that an admission controller raises by N per second based on booking-tier health (p99 latency, error rate, active checkouts). Clients poll the now-serving value, a single object cacheable at the edge for a second or two, so a million pollers cost almost nothing. When a fan's number comes up they get a signed token valid for about 10 minutes and bound to their account, with one active token per account. Bots are handled by pre-registration (Ticketmaster's Verified Fan), a CAPTCHA at queue entry, per-account ticket limits and rate limits per account, device and IP (Chapter 11). When inventory runs out, close the queue and tell everyone right away.

In the interview
Separate correctness from load
Say it explicitly: correctness comes from conditional writes (a hold is a compare-and-set with an expiry, and the unique constraint on (show_id, seat_id) is the last guard), while load is handled by the waiting room, so the booking tier sees hundreds of writes per second, not a million. Then walk through the late-payment race before the interviewer asks. What if payment succeeds after the hold expires? is the most common follow-up on this problem.
