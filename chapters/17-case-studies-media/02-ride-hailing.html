Design a ride-hailing service (Uber / Ola)

Ride-hailing looks like one system but contains two with opposite needs. Driver locations are a firehose: hundreds of thousands of tiny writes per second, each worthless 30 seconds later, where losing one is harmless. Trips are a trickle, around a thousand new trips per second at peak, where every change must be right: a driver is never assigned two riders and a fare is charged exactly once. A strong answer stores and scales these two paths differently and spends its deep-dive time on the geospatial index and the matching guarantee. Rides are local, so the whole system partitions naturally by city.

1. Requirements

  • Rider: see nearby cars, get an upfront fare with a pickup ETA, request a ride, track the driver live, pay automatically at the end.
  • Driver: go online, stream location, receive ride offers and accept or decline within ~15 s, navigate to pickup and drop-off.
  • Trip lifecycle: requested, matched, arrived, in progress, completed, with cancellations and no-shows.
  • Pricing: surge multipliers when demand outstrips supply in an area. Out of scope: ratings, pooled rides, scheduled rides.
  • Matching latency: a first offer within a few seconds of the request; time-to-match usually under ~30 s.
  • Freshness: driver positions at most ~5 s old in the index and on the rider's map.
  • Correctness: one driver per trip and one trip per driver; the price shown is the price charged; payment exactly once.
  • Availability: the request path survives losing a zone, and an outage in one city doesn't spread to others.
  • Scale: millions of online drivers worldwide, with sharp local peaks at concerts, in rain, on New Year's Eve.

2. Estimates

text
Assume 2M drivers online at global peak, one location update every 4 s
  location writes   2M / 4 s                       = 500k updates/s
  payload ~100 B    500k x 100 B                   = 50 MB/s ingest
  history (avg 1M online) 250k/s x 100 B x 86,400  ~ 2.2 TB/day
Live index
  2M drivers x ~200 B (id, cell, lat, lng, status, vehicle) ~ 400 MB of RAM
Riders looking at the map (3M apps open at peak, refresh every 5 s)
  3M / 5 s                                         = 600k nearby-driver reads/s
Trips: assume 20M per day
  20M / 86,400                                     ~ 230 trips/s, ~1,200/s at a 5x peak
  ~10 state changes per trip                       ~ 12k trip writes/s at peak
  20M x 2 KB                                       = 40 GB/day, ~15 TB/year

The numbers dictate the storage split. 500k location writes/s of data with a 30-second shelf life belongs in memory, overwritten in place, with a Kafka log for anyone who needs history. A transactional database is the wrong home for it. The entire live index is only ~400 MB, so sharding is about write throughput and blast radius, not size. Trips at ~12k writes/s at peak are ordinary OLTP for a sharded relational store. Nearby-car reads (600k/s) come from the same in-memory index and can be approximated: the car icons on the map are only a hint, so serve them from a per-cell snapshot refreshed every couple of seconds.

5. High-level design

Location path. Each driver app holds one WebSocket to a gateway and sends a position every 4 s. The location service updates the in-memory geo index for that driver's city, moving the driver between cell sets if the cell changed, and appends the ping to Kafka, which feeds location history, the surge job and the live traffic speeds used for ETAs. Request path. The rider first gets a fare quote (pricing reads the surge multiplier for the pickup cell plus an ETA), then creates a trip with that quote ID and an idempotency key, so a double tap creates one trip. The trip service persists the trip as MATCHING and hands it to dispatch, which pulls available drivers from cells around the pickup, ranks them by road ETA, reserves one and pushes an offer through the gateway. On accept the trip becomes DRIVER_ASSIGNED, and the rider's app subscribes to that one driver's location stream until drop-off.

6. Deep dives

Deep dive A: the geospatial index. Chapter 14 covered geohash, quadtrees, S2 and H3; the question here is which suits a write-heavy set of moving points. A quadtree has to split and merge as drivers move. Cell-based schemes are better because an update is O(1): compute the cell ID from latitude and longitude, and if it changed, move the driver from the old cell's set to the new one. Uber uses H3, whose hexagons have six equidistant neighbors, so expanding a search ring by ring (a k-ring) and smoothing surge across neighbors behave uniformly in every direction. Resolution 9 (~0.1 km² per cell) suits dispatch and resolution 7 (~5 km²) suits surge. With geohash you must search the cell plus its 8 neighbors, because a driver 20 m away can sit across a cell boundary; a 6-character geohash is about 1.2 km × 0.6 km. Redis GEO (GEOADD/GEOSEARCH, which stores a 52-bit geohash as the sorted-set score) is a reasonable off-the-shelf answer for one city. Shard by city or region: matching never crosses cities, a hot city can be split into more shards by coarse cell, and a node failure affects one area for a few seconds, because every driver re-reports within 4 s and the node rebuilds itself from that.

python
RES = 9                                      # ~0.1 km2 hexagons
def on_location(driver_id, lat, lng, ts):
    d = drivers[driver_id]                   # created at login: status, product
    cell = h3.latlng_to_cell(lat, lng, RES)
    if d.cell != cell:                       # crossed into a new hexagon
        cells[d.cell].discard(driver_id)
        cells[cell].add(driver_id)
    d.cell, d.lat, d.lng, d.ts = cell, lat, lng, ts
    kafka.send("locations", key=driver_id, value=(lat, lng, ts))

def candidates(lat, lng, product, want=10, max_k=4):
    origin = h3.latlng_to_cell(lat, lng, RES)
    for k in range(1, max_k + 1):            # widen the search ring by ring
        found = [d for c in h3.grid_disk(origin, k) for d in cells[c]
                 if drivers[d].status == "AVAILABLE"
                 and drivers[d].product == product
                 and now() - drivers[d].ts < 30]    # skip stale drivers
        if len(found) >= want or k == max_k:
            return found                     # next: rank by road ETA, not distance

Deep dive B: matching, and the one-driver-one-trip guarantee. Rank candidates by ETA from the routing engine, not by straight-line distance; a driver across a river is close on the map but 15 minutes away by road. Then reserve before offering: an atomic compare-and-set on the driver's state, AVAILABLE to RESERVED for this trip with a version check, in the shard that owns that driver. If two dispatchers race for the same driver, exactly one CAS succeeds and the loser moves to its next candidate; nothing has to lock a whole city. The offer expires after ~15 s; a decline or timeout sets the driver back to AVAILABLE and the next candidate gets the offer. An accept moves the trip from MATCHING to DRIVER_ASSIGNED with a conditional update on the trip's version, so a late accept for a rider who already cancelled gets a 409. Greedy one-at-a-time matching is simple; in dense areas, batched matching (collect requests for 1–2 s and solve an assignment that minimizes total pickup time) gives better outcomes overall at the cost of a little latency.

Deep dive C: surge and ETA. Surge is a stream-processing job (Chapter 10). It consumes ride requests and app opens (demand) and available-driver counts (supply) per resolution-7 cell over a sliding window of a few minutes, turns the demand-to-supply ratio into a multiplier, smooths it across neighboring cells so the price doesn't jump at a street corner, and publishes it to Redis for the pricing service. A quote freezes the multiplier: the trip request references a quoteId that expires after a few minutes, so the price shown is the price charged, and the request stays idempotent. ETA comes from a routing engine over the road graph (precomputed shortcuts such as contraction hierarchies make city-scale shortest paths take milliseconds), fed with live speeds derived from the same location stream and corrected by a model trained on actual trip times.

In production
How Uber did it
Uber's dispatch system, as described in its 2015 engineering talks, used Google S2 cells as the unit of geographic sharding and Ringpop, an open-source library combining consistent hashing with SWIM-style gossip, to route each cell's work to the node that owned it. In 2018 Uber open-sourced H3, its hexagonal hierarchical index, which it uses for dispatch, pricing and analytics.
In the interview
What earns senior-level credit
Separate the two data paths out loud: locations are 500k writes/s of data that is worthless after 30 seconds, so they live in memory and a Kafka log; trips are ~12k writes/s at peak that must be correct, so they live in a transactional store with a state machine. Then show exactly where the one-driver-one-trip guarantee comes from (a CAS on driver state), rank by ETA rather than distance, and explain what happens when a city's geo shard dies: drivers re-report within 4 s and the shard rebuilds itself.
