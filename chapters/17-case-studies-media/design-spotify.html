Design Spotify (music streaming)

Design a music streaming service like Spotify. Users search a catalog of ~100 million tracks, play songs and podcasts instantly on phones, desktops and web, build and share playlists (including collaborative ones), download music for offline listening, and get personalized playlists. Artists are paid royalties based on how many times their tracks are streamed.

  • Which audio qualities do we offer, and does a free tier exist with lower quality and ads?
  • Is offline download in scope, and what DRM rules apply to downloaded tracks?
  • What counts as a stream for royalties, and how exact must those counts be?
  • Do collaborative playlists need real-time co-editing, or is eventual merging of edits fine?
  • Are podcasts and video podcasts in scope, or just music?

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: search and browse the catalog; play with under ~300 ms to the first sound and gapless transitions; playlists (create, reorder, share, collaborate); offline downloads; listening history; personalized playlists; per-stream royalty reporting.
  • Non-functional: 99.99% playback availability; offline plays still counted; royalty counts exact and auditable; catalog and metadata changes may propagate in minutes; bandwidth cost per listening hour kept low.

2. Estimates

text
Users: 250M DAU, 2 hours of listening per day
  250M x 120 min = 3 x 10^10 min/day / 1,440   ~ 21M concurrent streams, ~40M at peak
  average bitrate 160 kbps: 21M x 160 kbps     ~ 3.4 Tbps average, ~7 Tbps peak
  (compare ~40-80 Tbps for the video service in this chapter)
Catalog: 100M tracks x 3.5 min = 210 s each
  96 + 160 + 320 kbps = 576 kbps x 210 s       ~ 15 MB per track for all qualities
  100M x 15 MB                                 ~ 1.5 PB (small for object storage)
  head of the catalog: top 1M tracks x 4 MB    ~ 4 TB -> fits on one edge server's SSDs
Plays: 120 min / 3.5 min ~ 34 tracks per user per day
  250M x 34 = 8.5B plays/day / 86,400          ~ 100k plays/s, ~200k/s at peak
  ~3 events per play (start, 30 s mark, end or skip) -> ~300k events/s into Kafka
  8.5B x 3 events x 200 B                      ~ 5 TB/day of play events

Music is a much smaller delivery problem than video: an order of magnitude less egress, and the head of the catalog fits on every edge node. The hard parts move elsewhere: starting playback instantly, counting plays correctly for royalties (including plays that happen offline), and playlists edited by many people.

5. High-level design

To play a track, the app asks stream authorization for the file at the right quality. It gets back signed CDN URLs and fetches the content key from the key service, unless both are already cached locally. The audio itself comes from the CDN, and the API never carries audio bytes. Play events are batched by the app, buffered on disk while offline, and sent to an event gateway that writes them to Kafka. From there a streaming job updates approximate real-time counts, and the data lake feeds the exact daily royalty batch and the weekly recommendation batch, which writes personalized playlists back through the ordinary playlist service.

6. Deep dives

Instant, gapless playback. A 4 MB track doesn't need HLS-style segmenting: pick one quality per track from the network type and the user's setting, then fetch the file with HTTP range requests, first a small range so audio starts within a couple of hundred milliseconds, then the rest. Mid-track quality switching matters much less than for video because the whole file downloads in seconds. Prefetch the next track in the queue some seconds before the current one ends, so transitions are gapless and work through brief signal loss. Keep a client cache of several GB: people replay the same songs constantly, and a cache hit costs no bandwidth at all. Offline downloads are the same encrypted files plus content keys bound to the device, with an expiry that forces the device online periodically to re-check the subscription. Spotify requires this at least once every 30 days.

Counting streams for royalties. The rule is contractual: Spotify counts a stream once at least 30 seconds of the track have played. The client generates a unique play_id at the start of each play and sends events tagged with it. Offline plays sit in a local queue and arrive days later with their original timestamps. The pipeline is at-least-once from the client to Kafka, dedupes on play_id in the batch layer, and computes daily totals per track, country and product tier from the immutable log, so a statement can be recomputed and audited at any time. A separate fraud stage drops patterns such as accounts looping one track around the clock or bot farms pushing a release. Artist dashboards read approximate counts from the streaming job. That is the speed layer of the Lambda pattern in Chapter 10, while royalties come only from the batch layer.

Playlists edited by many people. Store a playlist as a header plus item rows, each with a stable item_id (the same song can appear twice) and a fractional position key. Inserting between keys a and b picks a key between them, so a move rewrites one row instead of renumbering 5,000. Every change is an operation (insert, remove, move, rename) applied against a base revision. If the base is stale, the server rebases the operation onto the newer revision. That is simple here, because operations reference item IDs rather than positions, so most concurrent edits commute, and an operation on an item someone deleted becomes a no-op. Clients sync with sinceRev and receive either the missing operations or a full snapshot. Personalized playlists are written once a week by a batch job, which is far cheaper than generating them on each request.

In production
How Spotify did it
Spotify's early desktop client used a peer-to-peer network to share part of the delivery load between listeners, and the company phased it out around 2014 as CDN capacity became cheap and listening moved to phones. Starting in 2016 Spotify moved its back end from its own data centers to Google Cloud Platform.

5Follow-up questions

  • A superstar drops an album at midnight and 30 million people press play at once. What happens at the CDN, the key service and the catalog cache?
  • How would you add a lossless tier at around 1 Mbps without breaking the cost model?
  • How do you make the next track start instantly on a bad mobile connection, and what does prefetching cost?
  • A label disputes last month's stream counts. How do you prove them?
  • How do you show friends' listening activity in near real time without fanning out every play event?
