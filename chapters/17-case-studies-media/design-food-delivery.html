Design a food-delivery app (Swiggy / DoorDash)

Design a food-delivery platform like Swiggy or DoorDash. Customers browse restaurants that can deliver to their location, order from menus and track the delivery live. Restaurants receive and accept orders on a tablet and set preparation times. Delivery partners (couriers) receive pickup offers on their phones. Demand spikes sharply at lunch and dinner.

45:00Mock interview timer. Start it when you begin talking.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • How many orders per day, how concentrated are they at meal peaks, and how many couriers are online at peak?
  • Does a restaurant set its own delivery radius, or does the platform decide serviceability dynamically?
  • Can a courier carry several orders at once (batching or stacking)?
  • When is payment authorized and when is it captured? What happens if the restaurant rejects or an item is out of stock?
  • How precise does the delivery ETA need to be, and is it a promise or an estimate?
  • Are grocery and multi-store orders in scope, or restaurant food only?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Start from the ride-hailing walkthrough: courier locations and the geo index carry over. What is different? There are three parties, the food has to be cooked, and couriers can carry several orders.
H2Restaurant discovery is a read-heavy geo query, and every customer in the same small area sees nearly the same candidate set. What can you precompute per H3 cell?
H3Don't send a courier the moment the order is placed. Dispatch so the courier arrives when the food is ready: ready time = acceptance time + predicted prep time.
H4Think of the order as a saga across payment, restaurant and courier, with compensations: void the authorization if the restaurant rejects, re-dispatch if the courier drops out.
H5Use Little's Law to size live tracking: orders per second at peak times average delivery time gives concurrent active orders.

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: location-aware restaurant feed and search; menus with live item availability; cart and checkout; restaurant accept or reject with a prep-time estimate; courier dispatch, pickup and drop-off; live tracking and ETA; cancellations and refunds.
  • Non-functional: checkout and order state are strongly consistent, with no lost or duplicate orders; the feed is fast (p99 under ~300 ms) but may be a minute stale; ETAs are accurate to within a few minutes; the system survives meal-peak spikes of 4–5x; failures stay contained to one city.

2. Estimates

text
Orders: 5M/day; the busiest dinner hour carries ~15%
  5M / 86,400                       ~ 58 orders/s average
  0.15 x 5M / 3,600 s               ~ 210 orders/s, ~400/s in the peak minute
  ~12 state changes per order       ~ 5k order writes/s at peak
Live tracking, by Little's Law (use the peak-hour rate: a peak minute doesn't last 35 min)
  210 orders/s x 35 min delivery = 210 x 2,100 s ~ 440k active orders at peak
Couriers: 500k online at peak, a ping every 5 s ~ 100k location writes/s
  customer tracking screens: 440k orders / 5 s ~ 90k courier positions/s pushed
Browsing: 30M DAU x 8 feed or search loads = 240M/day ~ 2.8k/s, ~12k/s at peak
  menus: 500k restaurants x 100 items x 1 KB    ~ 50 GB -> fits in a cache cluster

Orders are modest OLTP. The real load is geo work (courier pings, the per-cell feed, tracking pushes) and peak concentration. Sizing for the average gives about a quarter of the capacity the dinner rush needs.

5. High-level design

The feed reads the customer's H3 cell from the serviceability cache, filters it (open now, not overloaded) and ranks it, with menus served from cache. Checkout creates the order under an idempotency key and authorizes payment. The capture happens at delivery, so a rejected order costs nothing to unwind. The order service writes state and an outbox row in one transaction (Chapter 9). Events go to the restaurant tablet through a merchant gateway, to the dispatch optimizer, and to the tracking service, which pushes status and courier position to the customer over a WebSocket.

6. Deep dives

Serviceability: who can deliver here, right now. A plain radius search is wrong twice over: restaurants have irregular delivery zones (a river or a highway changes travel time), and a restaurant is only serviceable if couriers are nearby and it isn't swamped. Precompute the static part by rasterizing each delivery polygon into H3 resolution-8 cells (~0.7 km² each), giving a cell-to-restaurants index. Every minute or so, overlay the dynamic signals: open or closed, kitchen load, courier supply around the restaurant, rain. A 1,000 km² city has only ~1,400 such cells, and every customer in a cell shares the same candidate list, so the feed is a cache read plus a light personalized re-rank. Under heavy load the platform shrinks serviceability, temporarily dropping far-away restaurants from a cell, rather than accepting orders it can't deliver on time.

Dispatch timed to the food, with batching. The goal isn't the nearest courier now; it's a courier who arrives as the food comes out. Ready time = acceptance time + predicted prep time (a model over the restaurant's history, the items and the current queue). A courier sent too early waits at the counter, wasting supply at peak; one sent too late delivers cold food. Instead of greedy one-at-a-time matching, run an optimizer every 30–60 s per zone over all pending orders and nearby couriers. It minimizes a cost of lateness, courier idle time and food wait, and it may stack two orders onto one courier when they come from the same restaurant or lie along one route. Offers and acceptance reuse the ride-hailing machinery: a compare-and-set reservation on courier state, an offer timeout, and re-dispatch if the courier declines or stops moving toward the restaurant.

The order saga and its failure cases. Every transition is a conditional update on the order's version, published through the outbox, so a duplicate tablet tap or courier retry can't move an order backwards. Each failure has a defined compensation. Restaurant rejects or times out: void the authorization (after first escalating to a phone call to the restaurant). Item out of stock after acceptance: the customer approves a substitution, or the capture is reduced. Courier cancels after pickup: urgent re-dispatch to a courier near the current position. Customer cancels after cooking starts: charge per policy. Payment capture failure after delivery goes to a retry and collections queue and never blocks delivery. This is Chapter 8's orchestrated saga, with the order service as the orchestrator.

Tip
Peaks are scheduled; use that
Lunch and dinner happen every day, so scale on schedule: pre-warm gateways, dispatch and the serviceability job 30 minutes before the peak (Chapter 4's predictive scaling), and use surge delivery fees and courier incentives to move supply into the busy zones before the rush rather than during it.

5Follow-up questions

  • How would you predict prep time, and what happens to dispatch when the prediction is 15 minutes wrong?
  • A restaurant's tablet loses connectivity during the dinner peak. What does the system do with incoming orders?
  • How would you support scheduled orders placed for tomorrow's lunch?
  • How do you decide whether to stack a second order onto a courier who is already carrying one?
  • Rain starts across the whole city and demand doubles in ten minutes. Walk through what changes.
