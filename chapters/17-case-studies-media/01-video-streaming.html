Design a video streaming platform (YouTube / Netflix)

The prompt is usually Design YouTube or Design Netflix. They differ in one big way: YouTube ingests hundreds of hours of user uploads every minute, while Netflix serves a curated catalog it controls. They share a skeleton, though: accept a huge file, turn it into many formats, and deliver the bytes from a server as close to the viewer as possible. The decision that organizes everything else is splitting the control plane (metadata, auth, search, deciding which server a player should fetch from) from the data plane (video segments served by a CDN). Your application servers should never stream a single video byte.

1. Requirements

  • Upload: creators upload files up to several GB; an interrupted upload resumes instead of restarting.
  • Process: each upload becomes several resolutions and codecs, plus thumbnails, captions and a moderation verdict.
  • Watch: playback on phones, browsers and TVs with quality that adapts to the viewer's bandwidth; seek, and resume where you left off on another device.
  • Metadata and counts: title, description, channel, view and like counts. Search and recommendations exist but are out of scope beyond their interfaces.
  • Playback latency: first frame in under ~2 s; rebuffering is the quality metric users feel most.
  • Availability: 99.99% for playback; upload processing may lag by minutes without anyone noticing.
  • Durability: an acknowledged upload is never lost (object storage, Chapter 10).
  • Consistency: eventual is fine for counts and metadata propagation, but a video must never be playable before it passes moderation and access checks.
  • Cost: egress bandwidth dominates the bill, so every choice is judged by bytes delivered per dollar.

2. Estimates

  H.264 ladder ~10 Mbps + VP9/AV1 ladder ~6 Mbps = 16 Mbps = 2 MB/s = 7.2 GB/hour

  average video 10 min             -> ~4.3M new videos/day ~ 50 uploads/s

Viewing (assume 500M DAU, 40 min and 5 videos per user per day)
  500M x 40 min = 2 x 10^10 min/day / 1,440 min/day ~ 14M concurrent streams
  14M x 3 Mbps average bitrate     ~ 42 Tbps average, ~80 Tbps at evening peak
  page loads 2.5B/day / 86,400     ~ 29k/s, peak ~60k/s      (metadata API)
  6 s segments: 14M streams / 6 s  ~ 2.3M segment GETs/s     (all at the CDN)

Three conclusions fall out. Tens of terabits per second cannot leave a handful of data centers, so delivery must be a CDN, ideally one that sits inside ISP networks. Transcoding is a large, bursty batch workload that belongs on a job queue, never in the upload request. And the metadata API at ~60k QPS is an ordinary read-heavy service: cache it (Chapter 5), shard it (Chapter 7) and move on to the interesting parts.

5. High-level design

Write path. The client asks the Video API for an upload session and sends parts straight to object storage through presigned, resumable URLs (multipart upload, Chapter 10). The API tier never touches the bytes, and a dropped connection resumes from the last stored part. When the object is complete, storage emits an event to Kafka; the pipeline produces renditions and manifests, writes them to the segment store, and flips the video to READY only after moderation passes. Read path. The player calls the API for metadata and a short-lived signed manifest URL that already points at the best CDN nodes for this client. Everything after that, the manifests and thousands of segments, goes to the CDN edge, falling back through a regional tier and an origin shield to the segment store on a miss. Counts. Players send batched watch events to Kafka; a stream job dedupes by session, drops invalid and bot views, sums per video per few-second window and adds the deltas to a counter store. That is the ad-click aggregation pattern from Chapter 10, not an UPDATE views = views + 1 on a hot row.

6. Deep dives

Deep dive A: transcoding as a DAG. Encoding a 1-hour source serially into 12 renditions (6 resolutions × 2 codecs) takes hours on one machine. Instead the orchestrator builds a directed acyclic graph of small, independent tasks: probe and validate the file, split it at keyframes into ~10 s chunks (each chunk starts a new GOP, the group of pictures that depends on one keyframe, so it decodes on its own), encode every chunk × rendition pair in parallel, stitch each rendition back together, then package. One hour is 360 chunks × 12 renditions = 4,320 tasks; with a thousand workers, wall-clock time approaches the time to encode a single chunk. Audio, thumbnails, captions, moderation and copyright fingerprinting (YouTube's Content ID) run as parallel branches. A workflow engine persists the DAG state, so a crashed worker costs one task, not the whole job.

  • Idempotent tasks. Key every task by (video_id, chunk, rendition) and write its output to a deterministic object path, so retries and duplicate queue deliveries just overwrite the same file.
  • Fast path first. Publish as soon as 360p and 480p are ready and add 1080p and 4K as they finish, which is why a fresh upload briefly has no HD option.
  • Priorities. Separate queues so a creator's new upload never waits behind a bulk re-encode of the back catalog; run backfills on spare or spot capacity.
  • Per-title ladders. A cartoon needs far fewer bits than a football match at the same visual quality. Netflix moved from one fixed bitrate ladder to per-title encoding in 2015 and later to per-shot optimization.
  • Codec economics. VP9 and AV1 save roughly 30–50% bandwidth over H.264 but cost several times more compute to encode, so produce them only for videos with enough expected views. Google built a custom transcoding chip (the Argos VCU) for YouTube for exactly this reason.

Deep dive B: adaptive bitrate with HLS and DASH. Each rendition is cut into segments of a few seconds that start on a keyframe and are aligned across renditions, so the player can switch quality at any segment boundary. A manifest describes them. HLS (Apple) uses a master playlist that lists variants by BANDWIDTH and RESOLUTION, each pointing to a media playlist of segment URLs; DASH (an MPEG standard) uses one XML manifest called the MPD. With CMAF, a single set of fragmented-MP4 segments serves both manifest formats, so you store and cache one copy instead of two. The client picks the rendition, which keeps servers stateless. Throughput-based players estimate bandwidth from recent downloads and pick the highest bitrate below ~80% of it; buffer-based players (an approach Netflix published in 2014) choose by how many seconds are already buffered; production players such as ExoPlayer, AVPlayer, hls.js and dash.js blend the two. Start at a modest rendition so the first frame appears fast, then climb. Premium content is encrypted once with Common Encryption (the cbcs scheme) so the same files work with Widevine, PlayReady and FairPlay licenses.

In production
Deep dive C: CDN strategy, and how Netflix does it
Netflix runs its own CDN, Open Connect. It gives ISPs Open Connect Appliances, storage-heavy servers installed inside the ISP's network or at internet exchange points, and fills them during off-peak fill windows with the titles it predicts will be watched, so evening traffic is served from inside the viewer's own ISP. The control plane (accounts, catalog, and the steering service that tells each player which appliances to use) runs in AWS; the appliances only serve bytes. YouTube similarly places Google Global Cache nodes inside ISPs, but its unbounded user-generated catalog relies much more on caching on demand.

Pre-position (push) at the edge

Strengths
  • Near-100% edge hit ratio for a predictable catalog
  • Fills use cheap off-peak bandwidth
  • ISPs save transit, so they agree to host the boxes
Weaknesses
  • Needs a good popularity forecast
  • Hardware and operations in hundreds of ISPs
  • Useless for a video uploaded five minutes ago
Use whenCurated, finite catalogs with predictable demand: Netflix, Disney+, scheduled new releases.

Pull-through tiered caching

Strengths
  • Works for an unbounded catalog with no forecasting
  • Viral videos warm the edges automatically
  • Commercial CDNs offer it out of the box
Weaknesses
  • The first viewer at each edge pays a miss
  • Low hit ratio on the long tail
  • Needs an origin shield so miss storms don't hit storage
Use whenUser-generated long tail and live streams; combine it with push for the most popular head of the catalog.
In the interview
How to frame it
Lead with the split: APIs serve metadata and signed manifest URLs; every byte of video comes from the CDN. My estimate is ~40 Tbps of egress, so the CDN strategy is the design. Then draw upload as a DAG of idempotent tasks, explain ABR as a client decision over aligned segments, and say how counts are aggregated. Candidates lose points for streaming video through app servers, transcoding inside the upload request, or incrementing a counter row on every view.
