Failover and split brain

When a follower fails, recovery is easy: it restarts, reads the last log position it applied, and catches up from the leader. When the leader fails, one follower must become the new leader, clients must start sending writes to it, and the other followers must replicate from it. That is failover, and most replication incidents happen during it.

  1. Detect the failure
    Nodes exchange heartbeats. If the leader misses them for a timeout (commonly 10 to 30 seconds), it is declared dead. There is no reliable way to tell a crashed node from a slow one or an unreachable one.
  2. Choose a new leader
    Prefer the replica with the most recent data (the highest replayed log position), and with semi-sync, one that acknowledged every commit. The choice should come from a majority vote or a consensus store, not from a single watcher process.
  3. Reroute clients
    Update whatever clients use to find the leader: a virtual IP, a DNS record, a proxy such as HAProxy or ProxySQL, or service discovery. Other followers start replicating from the new leader.
  4. Fence the old leader
    Make sure the old leader cannot keep accepting writes if it comes back: it must find out that it was demoted and rejoin as a follower, discarding any writes the new leader never received.

Tools automate this. Patroni keeps a PostgreSQL leader key with a TTL in etcd, Consul or ZooKeeper; a primary that cannot renew the key demotes itself. Orchestrator does topology management and failover for MySQL. MongoDB replica sets elect a new primary themselves, typically within about 12 seconds with the default 10-second election timeout. Amazon RDS Multi-AZ fails over to a synchronous standby, typically in one to two minutes.

What goes wrong

  • Lost writes. With async replication, the new leader may lack the old leader's last writes. When the old leader returns, its extra writes conflict with new ones and are usually discarded. MongoDB writes them to rollback files; w: "majority" prevents acknowledged writes from being rolled back.
  • Broken references outside the database. If auto-increment IDs are reissued after a lossy failover, caches, search indexes or other services that stored the old IDs now point at the wrong rows.
  • Timeout tuning. Too short a timeout causes unnecessary failovers during load spikes or GC pauses, and a failover adds load at the worst moment. Too long a timeout means a longer outage.
  • Split brain. Two nodes both believe they are the leader and both accept writes.
In production
GitHub, October 2018
A 43-second network partition between GitHub's US East Coast hub and its primary East Coast data center led its failover tooling, Orchestrator, to promote database primaries on the West Coast. Some writes accepted on the East Coast had not yet replicated, so each side had writes the other lacked. GitHub chose data integrity over a fast recovery and ran degraded for just over 24 hours while it restored and reconciled the data.

Preventing split brain

Split brain is dangerous because two leaders produce two divergent histories that usually cannot be merged automatically. Shorter timeouts make it more likely, not less. The real defenses are structural:

  • Majority-based leadership. Elect the leader through a quorum of at least three voters in different failure domains (etcd, ZooKeeper, or Raft inside the database). A node on the minority side of a partition cannot win or keep leadership.
  • Leases. Leadership is a lease that must be renewed with the majority every few seconds. A leader that cannot renew stops accepting writes before the lease expires, so by the time a new leader is chosen, the old one has stepped down.
  • Fencing. Every leadership change increments an epoch (Raft calls it a term). Replicas and storage reject anything stamped with an older epoch. In the bluntest form, STONITH ('shoot the other node in the head'), the cluster powers off the old leader. Fencing tokens are covered in Chapter 8.
In the interview
Walk through failover out loud
Cover detection (heartbeat timeout and why its value matters), election (most up-to-date replica, chosen by a quorum), rerouting (VIP, DNS or proxy) and fencing (epochs or leases so the old primary cannot write). Then state the RPO: 'with semi-sync, zero acknowledged writes lost; with async, up to the replication lag.'
