Monoliths, modular monoliths and microservices

A monolith is one deployable unit: one codebase, one build, one process type, usually one database. A microservice architecture splits the system into independently deployable services, each owning one business capability and its own data, talking over the network. Between them sits the modular monolith: one deployable, but with strictly enforced internal module boundaries, so each module could later become a service. Interviewers do not want to hear "microservices are modern, so microservices". They want the honest trade-off.

Monolith

Strengths
  • Simplest to build, test, debug and deploy
  • In-process calls: nanoseconds, no partial failure
  • ACID transactions across the whole domain
  • Refactoring across modules is a normal code change
Weaknesses
  • One team's bug or slow query can take down everything
  • Deploys get slow and risky as the codebase grows
  • Scales only as a whole: the image resizer and the login page get the same hardware
  • Boundaries erode into a big ball of mud without discipline
Use whenNew products, small teams (under ~20 to 30 engineers), and any domain you do not yet understand well.

Modular monolith

Strengths
  • Clear module ownership and enforced interfaces
  • Still one deploy and one transaction boundary
  • Cheap path to extract a module later
Weaknesses
  • Needs tooling to stop modules reaching into each other's tables
  • Still scales and fails as one unit
Use whenThe default for most growing companies; a good interview answer when the team is medium sized.

Microservices

Strengths
  • Teams deploy independently, many times a day
  • Scale and choose technology per service
  • Fault isolation when combined with timeouts and circuit breakers
Weaknesses
  • Every call can fail, time out or be slow: distributed systems problems everywhere
  • No cross-service transactions: sagas and eventual consistency
  • Heavy platform cost: discovery, tracing, CI/CD per service, on-call
  • Debugging a request means following it across 10 services
Use whenLarge organizations where team autonomy and deploy velocity are the bottleneck, with a mature platform team.

Decomposing by business capability and bounded context

Split by what the business does, not by technical layer. A "database service" and a "UI service" are layers; every feature change touches both, so you gain nothing. A business capability is something the business does that produces value: catalog, ordering, payments, shipping. A bounded context (from Domain-Driven Design) is the boundary within which a term has one precise meaning. "Order" means a cart with prices in Ordering, a parcel with a weight in Shipping, and a ledger entry in Accounting. Each context gets its own model, and services map to contexts.

  • High cohesion: things that change together live together. If two services must always be deployed together, they are one service.
  • Loose coupling: a service exposes a narrow API or events, never its tables.
  • Conway's law: systems mirror the communication structure of the teams that build them. Align one service, or a few, with one team that owns it end to end.
  • Avoid chatty boundaries: if rendering one page requires 40 synchronous calls between two services, the boundary is in the wrong place.

Database per service

Each service owns its data, and other services can reach it only through its API or the events it publishes. Shared databases look convenient but couple teams at the schema level: one team's migration breaks another team's queries, and nobody can change a column safely. Owning data also lets each service choose the right store: PostgreSQL for orders, Redis for sessions, Elasticsearch for catalog search.

Pitfall
The distributed monolith
The worst outcome is services that must be deployed together, share a database, or call each other synchronously in long chains. You get all the network failure modes of microservices with none of the independence. A chain of 5 synchronous services each at 99.9% availability is at best about 99.5% available, and its latency is the sum of all five.
In production
Both directions are real
Shopify runs one of the largest Ruby on Rails codebases as a modular monolith with enforced component boundaries. In 2023 Amazon's Prime Video team wrote that moving a video-quality monitoring tool from distributed serverless components to a single process cut its infrastructure cost by about 90%. Netflix, at the other extreme, runs hundreds of microservices with a large platform organization behind them.
In the interview
How to frame it
"I would start as a modular monolith and extract services where there is a concrete reason: a component with very different scaling needs, a team that is blocked on shared deploys, or a part with a different reliability requirement such as payments." Then name the boundary you would extract first and why. To migrate a live system, use the strangler fig pattern: put a routing layer in front, move one capability at a time behind it, and delete the old code path once traffic has moved.
