Lesson 1 of 7 · 8 min read

Sync vs async: what a queue buys you

Services talk in two basic ways. In synchronous communication (HTTP, gRPC) the caller sends a request and waits for the response; caller and callee must both be up at the same moment, and the caller's latency includes the callee's. In asynchronous communication the sender hands a message to a broker (RabbitMQ, Kafka, Amazon SQS) and moves on; a consumer picks it up later, possibly seconds later, possibly after a restart. The broker stores the message durably in between.

What a queue buys you

  • Decoupling. The producer does not know who consumes, how many consumers exist, or where they run. Adding a fraud-analytics consumer next month requires no change to checkout.
  • Buffering. If the email service is down for 20 minutes, messages wait in the broker instead of failing checkout. When it recovers, it works through the backlog.
  • Load leveling. A flash sale may produce 10,000 orders per second for two minutes, while the warehouse integration can only handle 1,000 per second. The queue absorbs the spike and the consumer drains it at a steady rate, so you size consumers for the average, not the peak.
  • Fan-out. One published event can feed many independent consumers, each at its own pace.
  • Retries for free. A message that fails processing stays in the broker and is redelivered, instead of the caller writing retry loops.

600kBacklog after 10 min of 2,000 msg/s in vs 1,000 msg/s out
~10 minTime to drain that backlog once input drops to 0 at 1,000 msg/s

The availability figure is the series rule: 0.999 to the fifth power is about 0.995, roughly 3.6 hours of downtime a month instead of 43 minutes. The backlog figure is the load-leveling arithmetic you should do aloud in an interview: backlog grows at (arrival rate minus processing rate) and drains at (processing rate minus arrival rate). If the backlog can exceed what the broker retains, or the resulting delay is unacceptable, you need more consumers, not just a queue.

What it costs

CostWhyMitigation
No immediate answerThe caller gets 'accepted', not the resultReturn 202 with a status resource, push the result by WebSocket or a callback
Eventual consistencyDownstream state lags the producer by milliseconds to minutesDesign the UI for pending states; read your own writes from the source
Duplicates and reorderingBrokers redeliver after failures; parallel consumers finish out of orderIdempotent consumers, partition keys for ordering (lessons 3 and 5)
Harder debuggingA request becomes a chain of messages across servicesCorrelation IDs in message headers, distributed tracing (Chapter 13)
Another system to runThe broker needs capacity planning, monitoring and upgradesManaged services: Amazon MSK, Confluent Cloud, SQS
Pitfall
Don't queue what the user is waiting for
If the user needs the result to continue (login, 'is this seat available', a search query), keep the call synchronous and make it fast. Putting a queue in the middle of a request the user is blocked on just adds latency and failure modes. Queue the work that can happen after the response: emails, thumbnails, indexing, analytics, payouts.
In the interview
How to justify a queue
Name the specific benefit: 'Sending the receipt is not on the critical path, so checkout publishes OrderPlaced and the email worker consumes it. If the email provider is down, orders still succeed and emails catch up.' Then name the cost you accept: 'the email may arrive a few seconds late, and the worker must deduplicate because delivery is at least once.'
