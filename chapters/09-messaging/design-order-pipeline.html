Design an e-commerce order processing pipeline

Design the backend pipeline that runs after a customer clicks 'Place order' on a large e-commerce site: reserve inventory, charge payment, notify the customer, hand off to the warehouse and feed analytics. Traffic spikes 20x during sales. No order may be lost or charged twice.

45:00Mock interview timer. Start it when you begin talking.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • How many orders per day, and how big is the sale-day peak?
  • Must the customer see 'confirmed' synchronously, or is 'received, confirming' acceptable?
  • Which steps must happen in order for one order, and which can run in parallel?
  • Is the payment provider synchronous, and does it support idempotency keys?
  • How long may a downstream system (warehouse, email) be down before it is a business problem?
  • Do analytics need every event, and how fresh?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Separate what the customer waits for (order accepted) from what can happen afterwards (payment capture, emails, warehouse).
H2The order service must save the order and emit an event atomically. Which pattern avoids dual writes?
H3Which key keeps one order's events in order without creating hot partitions?
H4Delivery is at least once. How does the payment consumer avoid charging twice?
H5What happens to a message the warehouse API rejects, and to messages during a 20-minute warehouse outage?

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

Requirements

  • Functional: accept an order; reserve stock; authorize and capture payment; confirm or cancel; notify the customer; send to the warehouse; stream events to analytics; expose order status.
  • Non-functional: never lose an accepted order; never double charge; order acceptance p99 under 300 ms even at peak; downstream outages must not block acceptance; per-order events processed in order; end-to-end confirmation typically within 5 seconds.

Estimates

  • 10M orders/day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 120 orders/s average; sale peak 20x ≈ 2,400 orders/s, round to 3,000/s for headroom.
  • Each order produces ~8 events (placed, stock reserved, payment authorized, confirmed, picked, shipped, and so on): 3,000 × 8 = 24,000 events/s at peak. At ~1.5 KB each that is ~36 MB/s into Kafka, easy for a small cluster.
  • Kafka storage: 10M × 8 × 1.5 KB = 120 GB/day; × 7 days × RF 3 ≈ 2.5 TB.
  • Partitions: payment authorization takes ~200 ms at the provider, so a consumer handling one record at a time manages only 5/s. Each payment consumer therefore processes records for different order_ids concurrently (up to 50 in flight, still sequential per key, committing only the lowest fully processed offset): ~250/s per consumer. Peak ≈ 3,000 payment events/s ÷ 250 = 12 consumers; 48 partitions per topic leaves 4x headroom.

Data model

Each service owns its data. Orders and inventory live in PostgreSQL (transactions, constraints, conditional updates); events flow through Kafka topics keyed by order_id.

High-level design

Write path: the order service validates the cart, then in one PostgreSQL transaction inserts the order (status RECEIVED) and an OrderPlaced outbox row, and returns 202. Debezium publishes the outbox row to order-events. Inventory reserves stock and emits StockReserved or StockUnavailable; payment authorizes the card on StockReserved and emits PaymentAuthorized or PaymentFailed; the order service consumes those results and moves the order to CONFIRMED or CANCELLED, emitting further events through the same outbox. Notification, warehouse and analytics each consume with their own consumer group. Read path: GET reads the orders table, which always reflects the latest consumed result.

Deep dives

1. Ordering and the key. All events are keyed by order_id, so one order's lifecycle stays in one partition per topic and each consumer of order-events sees OrderPlaced before OrderCancelled for the same order. There is no ordering across topics, which is why the order state machine must tolerate results arriving in any order. customer_id would also preserve order but creates hot partitions for marketplace sellers or B2B buyers. Partition counts are fixed at 48 up front, because adding partitions later remaps keys.

2. No double charge. Delivery is at least once end to end. The payment service records each consumed event ID in processed_events in the same transaction as the payment row, and payments.order_id is unique. It calls the provider with Idempotency-Key = order_id, so even if the service crashes after charging but before recording, the retried call returns the original charge rather than creating a new one.

3. Flash sale load leveling. Acceptance is one small transaction, so the order service scales horizontally and absorbs 3,000 orders/s. Downstream consumers drain at their own rate. If payments fall behind by 30 seconds, customers simply see RECEIVED a little longer. Alert on lag measured in seconds per consumer group, and autoscale consumers up to the partition count. Inventory must be strongly consistent per SKU: the reservation is a conditional update (UPDATE stock SET available = available - :n WHERE sku = :sku AND available >= :n), and hot SKUs in a sale can be split into several stock buckets to reduce row contention.

4. Failures. Provider timeouts and 5xx responses go to retry-1m, then retry-15m, then the DLQ with full error context; validation errors go straight to the DLQ. The warehouse connector simply stops committing during a warehouse outage: Kafka retains 7 days of events, so a 20-minute outage is just lag that clears afterwards. Orders whose payment never resolves are cancelled by a timeout check, which releases the stock reservation (a compensation, as in the Chapter 8 sagas).

5Follow-up questions

  • How would you show the customer 'confirmed' within 2 seconds most of the time without making checkout synchronous?
  • Payments is down for an hour during a sale. What does the customer see, and what happens to inventory reservations?
  • How do you replay a week of order events into a new fraud-detection service without disturbing existing consumers?
