SLIs, SLOs, error budgets and alerting

"The system should be highly available" is not a requirement anyone can test. Google's SRE practice turned it into three precise terms, and interviewers expect you to use them correctly.

TermWhat it isExampleWho it is for
SLI, service level indicatorA measured ratio of good events to valid eventsFraction of GET /feed requests answered with non-5xx in under 300 msEngineers: the number on the dashboard
SLO, service level objectiveA target for an SLI over a time window99.9% of feed requests are good, over a rolling 30 daysThe team: the bar you engineer and operate to
SLA, service level agreementA contract with consequences if missed99.5% monthly availability or the customer gets a 10% service creditCustomers and lawyers; set looser than the SLO

Good SLIs measure what users experience, as close to them as possible: load balancer logs or client telemetry rather than a server's own view. Typical SLIs are availability (good requests / valid requests), latency (fraction of requests faster than a threshold, which is easier to reason about than a raw p99), freshness for pipelines (fraction of data updated within 5 minutes) and durability for storage. Exclude errors the client caused, such as most 4xx responses.

Error budgets

The error budget is the unreliability the SLO allows: 1 − SLO. For a 99.9% SLO the budget is 0.1%. An average month has 30.44 days × 1,440 = 43,830 minutes, so 0.001 × 43,830 ≈ 43.8 minutes of full downtime a month (43.2 minutes if you use a 30-day window). For a request-based SLO the budget is a count: with 100 million requests a month, 99.9% allows 100,000 failed requests. A 20-minute incident at 50% errors uses 10 minutes' worth, about 23% of the month's budget.

The budget turns reliability into a shared, spendable resource. While budget remains, the team ships features, runs experiments and does risky migrations. When it is spent, an agreed error budget policy applies: freeze non-critical releases and put engineers on reliability work until the window recovers. This ends the argument between "ship faster" and "don't break things" with data. Note also that 100% is the wrong target: users cannot tell 99.999% from 100% through their own flaky Wi-Fi, and each extra nine costs roughly 10x more (Chapter 12 shows how dependencies in series cap you anyway).

Alert on symptoms, not causes

  • Symptoms are what users feel: error rate up, latency up, data stale. These page a human, because they mean the SLO is at risk.
  • Causes are internal conditions: CPU at 90%, one replica down, a queue growing. Many are harmless (a node at 90% CPU serving fine), and others get missed because nobody wrote the alert. Show them on dashboards and file tickets; do not page on them.
  • Exceptions: page on causes that predict an outage nobody will see coming from symptoms, such as a disk that will be full in 4 hours or a certificate that expires within a day (a cert 30 days from expiry is a ticket).
  • Every page must be urgent, user-impacting and actionable, with a runbook link. A team that gets 50 pages a week stops reading them; this is alert fatigue.

Burn-rate alerts

The naive alert "error rate above 0.1% for 5 minutes" pages on every small blip and is slow to catch large ones. Alert on burn rate instead: how fast you are spending the budget relative to the steady rate that would use it up exactly at the end of the window. A burn rate of 1 uses a 30-day budget in 30 days; a burn rate of 14.4 uses it in 720 / 14.4 = 50 hours, which means 2% of the budget in a single hour. The Google SRE Workbook recommends pairing a long window (to be significant) with a short window (so the alert stops soon after the problem does):

ActionLong windowShort windowBurn rateBudget consumed in long window
Page1 hour5 minutes14.42%
Page6 hours30 minutes65%
Ticket3 days6 hours110%
text
# Page when checkout burns its 99.9% budget 14.4x too fast,
# measured over both the last 1 hour and the last 5 minutes.
(
  sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout", code=~"5.."}[1h]))
  / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[1h]))
) > (14.4 * 0.001)
and
(
  sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout", code=~"5.."}[5m]))
  / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout"}[5m]))
) > (14.4 * 0.001)

Dashboards

  • Top level: one SLO dashboard per user journey (sign-in, feed, checkout) showing SLI, target and budget remaining.
  • Per service: RED metrics per endpoint, plus deploy markers so you can see whether a release lines up with a change.
  • Per resource: USE metrics for databases, caches, queues and hosts, for drilling into causes.
  • Keep dashboards as code (Grafana JSON or Terraform) in version control so they stay consistent across services.
In the interview
Bring SLOs into every design
In the non-functional requirements, say "99.9% of feed reads succeed in under 300 ms over 30 days, so about 43 minutes of budget a month." In the wrap-up, say what you would measure (RED per service, the SLI at the load balancer) and that you would page on burn rate, not CPU. It costs you 30 seconds and signals production experience.
