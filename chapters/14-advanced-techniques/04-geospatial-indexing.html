Geospatial indexing and nearby search

"Find restaurants within 2 km" looks like a simple filter: WHERE lat BETWEEN ? AND ? AND lng BETWEEN ? AND ?. The problem is that a B-tree indexes one dimension well. An index on (lat, lng) narrows by latitude and then scans every place in that latitude band around the whole planet, a strip 4 km tall and 40,000 km long, to filter by longitude. Two separate indexes are no better: each returns a huge band and the database has to intersect them. Geospatial indexes fix this by mapping 2D space onto cells with IDs that a normal index or key-value store can look up, so that points that are close together usually get nearby IDs.

Geohash

A geohash recursively halves the world. The first bit says whether the longitude is in the east or west half, the second bit does the same for latitude, and the bits keep alternating, each one halving the cell. Every 5 bits become one character of a base32 alphabet (0-9 and b-z without a i l o). A point in central San Francisco, 37.7749, −122.4194, encodes to 9q8yyk8yt. The key property is the prefix: every point inside the cell 9q8yyk has a geohash starting with 9q8yyk, so "everything in this cell" is a B-tree range scan, WHERE geohash >= '9q8yyk' AND geohash < '9q8yym', on a plain string column in PostgreSQL, DynamoDB or Cassandra. That works because the base32 alphabet is in ASCII order, so string order equals bit order.

Each extra character divides a cell by 32, alternating between 8 × 4 and 4 × 8 splits, which is why the shape flips between square and 2:1. East-west widths also shrink with latitude by cos(latitude): at 60° north a precision-6 cell is about 0.6 km wide instead of 1.2 km.

The edge problem and the 3 × 3 search

Shared prefixes mean nearness, but nearness does not mean a shared prefix. Two points 10 m apart on either side of the Greenwich meridian (51.4779, +0.00007 and 51.4779, −0.00007) encode to u10hb5 and gcpuzg: no common characters at all, because they sit on opposite sides of the very first split. A user standing near the edge of their cell is also closer to places in the next cell than to most places in their own. So a nearby search never queries just the user's cell. It queries the cell plus its 8 neighbours, then computes the true distance (haversine) for every candidate and drops those outside the radius.

Choose the precision so the cell's shorter side is at least the search radius. Then any point within the radius is at most one cell away in each direction, so the 3 × 3 block is guaranteed to contain all of them. For a 500 m search, precision 6 (short side about 600 m) works; for 1 km you must drop to precision 5, because a precision-6 block only guarantees about 600 m of coverage.

Quadtrees

Geohash cells are a fixed grid, but places are not spread evenly. A precision-5 cell in Manhattan may hold tens of thousands of businesses while one in Nevada holds none. A quadtree adapts to density instead: start with one node covering the whole map, and whenever a node holds more than a capacity K (say 100 places), split it into four equal quadrants and push its places down. Dense areas end up with many small leaves and empty areas with a few huge ones, so every leaf has at most K places to scan.

A quadtree usually lives in memory on each search server. Sizing for 200M places with K = 100: at least 2M leaves (more in practice, since leaves are partly full). Each place costs an 8-byte ID plus 16 bytes of latitude and longitude for distance checks, so 200M × 24 bytes ≈ 4.8 GB, plus a few hundred MB of node overhead: about 5 GB, which fits in one server's memory. To search, find the leaf containing the user, then visit neighbouring leaves in order of distance until you have enough results. The costs: building the tree from 200M rows takes minutes, so servers need a warm-up period and a rolling rebuild, and live updates mean splitting and merging nodes under concurrent reads. Many systems just rebuild nightly and accept that a new restaurant appears the next day.

Google S2 and Uber H3

Geohash

Strengths
  • Simple, plain string keys
  • Prefix query is a range scan in any ordered store
  • Supported by Redis GEO, Elasticsearch, many libraries
Weaknesses
  • Fixed grid ignores density
  • Edge discontinuities need the 3 × 3 trick
  • Cell shapes distort toward the poles
Use whenDefault for proximity search on a key-value store or relational table.

Quadtree

Strengths
  • Adapts to density: bounded points per leaf
  • Natural k-nearest-neighbour search
Weaknesses
  • In-memory structure to build, update and replicate
  • Not a storage key format by itself
Use whenIn-memory index for a read-heavy service with very uneven density.

Google S2

Strengths
  • Projects the sphere onto 6 cube faces, so cells distort far less than lat/lng grids
  • 64-bit cell IDs ordered along a Hilbert curve, which preserves locality better than geohash's Z-order
  • Levels 0 to 30, down to about 1 cm²; any region is covered by a few cells of mixed levels
Weaknesses
  • More complex library and concepts
  • Cells are quadrilaterals, so neighbour distances still vary
Use whenPrecise coverings of arbitrary regions and polygons, such as delivery zones or geofences.

Uber H3

Strengths
  • Hexagonal cells: all 6 neighbours are the same distance away
  • 16 resolutions; k-ring neighbourhoods are easy and uniform
Weaknesses
  • Children do not exactly tile the parent, so the hierarchy is approximate
  • 12 pentagons per resolution are special cases
Use whenAggregating and smoothing over areas: supply and demand, surge pricing, heat maps.

S2 converts a search circle into a covering: a small set of cells of mixed sizes that together contain the circle (S2's region coverer aims for 8 cells by default). Each cell is a contiguous range of 64-bit IDs, so the query becomes a handful of range scans. H3 is the natural choice when the question is about areas rather than points, because hexagons have one kind of neighbour instead of the square grid's edge and corner neighbours. At resolution 9 an H3 hexagon averages about 0.1 km².

In production
Off-the-shelf geo indexes
Redis stores GEOADD members in a sorted set whose score is a 52-bit geohash, and GEOSEARCH (Redis 6.2+) checks the surrounding cells and filters by distance. MongoDB's 2dsphere index is built on S2 cells. PostGIS answers ST_DWithin queries using an R-tree-style GiST index. Uber open-sourced H3 in 2018 after using hexagons to analyse supply, demand and pricing across cities.
In the interview
The interview move for nearby search
Explain why a plain lat/lng index fails, pick geohash (or S2) for storage keys, and then say the three details interviewers check: query the 8 neighbouring cells, re-rank candidates by true distance, and handle density by expanding to a coarser precision when results are too few, or switch to a quadtree when density varies wildly. Also note that a store with 200M places is only a few GB of index: it fits in memory, so scale reads with replicas rather than sharding.
