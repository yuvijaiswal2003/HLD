Requirements: turning a vague prompt into numbers

Interview prompts are vague on purpose: “Design Twitter.” Your first job is to turn that into a short written list of what the system must do and how well it must do it. Everything you design later should trace back to this list.

Functional requirements

Functional requirements are the features: behavior a user or another system can observe. Phrase them as actions: “a user can post a message of up to 280 characters”, “a user sees a timeline of posts from accounts they follow, newest first”. In an interview, pick the 3 to 5 core features and say explicitly what is out of scope, such as ads, direct messages or analytics.

Non-functional requirements

Non-functional requirements (NFRs, also called quality attributes) describe how well the system performs those functions. They shape the architecture far more than features do. A chat app for 100 users and one for a billion have the same features and completely different designs.

LatencyHow long a request takesPercentiles such as p50 and p99 per key operationTimeline p99 under 200 ms
ConsistencyWhether readers see the latest writeWhich data must be strongly consistent and how stale the rest may beLike counts may lag 5 s; account balances never

A note on latency: the p99 (99th percentile) is the time that 99% of requests beat. Prefer percentiles to averages, because an average hides the slow tail. If a page makes 50 backend calls, the chance that at least one is slower than the backend's p99 is 1 - 0.99^50, about 40%, so tail latency shapes what users actually feel.

Turning vague asks into numbers

  1. Ask who and how many
    “How many daily active users (DAU) should we design for, and where are they?” Example answer: 10 million DAU, mostly North America and Europe.
  2. Ask what they do
    “How many times a day does a typical user read and write?” Example: views 20 posts and writes 1, so reads outnumber writes 20 to 1.
  3. Ask about the data
    “How big is a typical object, and how long do we keep it?” Example: 1 KB per post, kept forever, with optional images.
  4. Ask what matters most
    “Which is worse here: showing slightly stale data, losing data, or being down?” The answer tells you where to spend on consistency, durability and availability.
  5. Write it down and convert
    Turn the answers into requests per second, storage per year and bandwidth. Chapter 3 shows how to do this arithmetic quickly.

Here is what a finished requirements list looks like for “Design a comments system for a news site.” Notice how short it is. It fits in a corner of the whiteboard and drives every later decision.

text
Functional
  - Post a comment on an article (text, up to 2,000 chars)
  - List comments for an article, newest first, paginated
  - Reply to a comment (one level of nesting)
  - Out of scope: ML moderation, notifications, reactions

Non-functional
  - 5M DAU, reads:writes about 100:1
  - Read p99 under 200 ms, post p99 under 500 ms
  - 99.9% availability for reads
  - New comments may take up to 5 s to appear for other readers
  - An acknowledged comment is never lost
In the interview
Plant your trade-offs early
Say the consistency decision while gathering requirements: “I'll assume a few seconds of delay before other readers see a new comment is fine, but the comment itself must never be lost.” Later, when you add caches and async replication, you can point back to this agreement.
Pitfall
Don't interrogate the interviewer
Four to seven focused questions is plenty. If the interviewer says “you decide”, state a reasonable assumption out loud (“I'll assume 10M DAU”) and move on. Spending 15 minutes on questions is as harmful as asking none.
