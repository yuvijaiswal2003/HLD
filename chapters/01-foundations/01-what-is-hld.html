What system design is, and what interviewers grade

System design is deciding which components a software system needs, what each one is responsible for, how they talk to each other, and where the data lives, so that the whole thing meets its goals for scale, speed, reliability and cost. A high-level design (HLD) works at the level of boxes and arrows: clients, load balancers, application services, databases, caches, queues, and the requests and data flowing between them.

A low-level design (LLD) zooms into one of those boxes: the classes and interfaces inside a service, function signatures, the exact columns and indexes of a table, error handling, design patterns. Real engineering needs both. Interviews usually test them in separate rounds, and this course is about HLD.

Why there is no single right answer

Every design is a bundle of trade-offs. Take the like counter on a post. Keeping it in one SQL row is simple and always exact, but that row becomes a lock hotspot when a viral post gets 50,000 likes per second. Splitting it into many counter rows (or keeping it in an in-memory store and flushing periodically) scales, but the number you show can be slightly behind for a moment. Neither is “correct” in the abstract. The right choice depends on how exact the count must be and how much traffic you expect.

Interviewers know this. They are not checking whether your diagram matches a reference answer. They are checking whether you can name the options, pick one for reasons tied to the requirements, and say what you gave up.

What interviewers actually evaluate

SignalDone wellRed flag
Problem scopingAsks focused questions, writes down requirements and scale, agrees on what is out of scopeStarts drawing boxes in the first minute
StructureMoves through clear phases and says what comes nextWanders between topics, revisits basics at minute 40
Technical depthExplains how a component works and why it fits this workloadNames products without explaining them
Trade-offsCompares two or more options against the requirementsPresents one option as the only possible one
Quantitative reasoningUses estimates to justify decisions such as shard count or cache sizeNever computes anything, or computes and then ignores the result
CommunicationThinks out loud, checks in, adapts to hintsGoes silent for minutes, or argues with every hint

Expectations rise with level. A mid-level candidate should reach a working, sensible design with some prompting. A senior candidate should drive the conversation, find the hard parts without being told, and go deep on two or three of them. A staff-level candidate additionally discusses operations (deploys, migrations, on-call), how the design evolves over years, and its cost to the organization.

In the interview
One sentence that shows four signals
“I'll use PostgreSQL for orders because we need multi-row transactions, and at about 2,000 writes per second a single primary handles it. If writes grow 10x, we'd shard by customer_id.” That sentence states a requirement, a decision, a number, and an escape hatch. Aim to talk like this all interview.
Tip
Treat hints as directions
When an interviewer asks “what happens if this node dies?” or “how big does this table get?”, they are pointing at the part they want you to go deep on. Thank them, answer it properly, and keep going.
