Part 2 / Ch 4 · Scaling and Load Balancing
Lesson 6 of 6 · 11 min read

Autoscaling

Autoscaling adds and removes instances automatically so capacity follows demand. It saves money at night, absorbs daily peaks without human action, and replaces failed instances. It works cleanly only for stateless tiers; scaling stateful tiers (databases, Kafka brokers) involves moving data and is usually a deliberate operation, not a reflex.

What to scale on

MetricGood forPitfall
CPU utilizationCPU-bound web and API serversUseless for I/O-bound services that wait on downstream calls
Requests per instanceHTTP tiers with a known per-instance capacity from load tests (ALB RequestCountPerTarget)Assumes requests cost about the same
Latency (p95/p99)Safety net when users are already feeling itLagging signal; slow downstream dependencies trigger useless scale-out
Queue depth / backlog per workerAsync workers reading SQS or Kafka (KEDA in Kubernetes)Needs a target: acceptable backlog = rate x tolerable delay
Concurrent connectionsWebSocket and streaming gatewaysIdle connections still cost memory, not CPU

Scaling policies

  • Target tracking: keep a metric at a set point, like a thermostat (AWS target tracking, the Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler). The usual first choice.
  • Step scaling: fixed actions at thresholds, for example add 10% when CPU is above 70% and 30% when above 85%.
  • Scheduled scaling: set capacity by the clock for known events: business hours, a marketing email at 9:00, a sale at noon.
  • Predictive scaling: the platform learns daily and weekly patterns from recent history and launches capacity before the forecast rise (AWS EC2 Auto Scaling offers this).

Kubernetes HPA rule (target tracking):
  desiredReplicas = ceil( currentReplicas * currentMetric / targetMetric )

Target CPU 60%, 10 pods averaging 90%:
  desired = ceil(10 * 90 / 60) = 15 pods

Next check, 15 pods averaging 58%:
  ratio 58/60 = 0.97 is inside the default 10% tolerance, so no change

Cooldowns and flapping

After a scaling action, metrics take time to reflect the new capacity: instances must boot and warm up, and metrics are aggregated over minutes. Without a pause, the scaler sees the old high CPU and adds even more, or scales in, sees CPU jump, and scales out again. This oscillation is called flapping. Cooldowns and stabilization windows prevent it: the Kubernetes HPA waits 300 seconds by default before acting on a lower recommendation, and AWS simple scaling has a 300-second default cooldown. The rule of thumb is scale out fast, scale in slowly, because under-capacity hurts users while over-capacity only costs money.

Cold starts and warm pools

Autoscaling is never instant. The real delay is the sum of several steps, and it is usually minutes, not seconds:

1–3 minDetect: metric aggregation plus alarm evaluation periods
30–90 sLaunch and boot a new VM
10 s to minutesApp start, JIT warm-up, cache fill, readiness checks
SecondsStart a container on a node that already has room

To shorten the gap, keep pre-initialized capacity on hand. EC2 Auto Scaling warm pools hold instances that have already booted and run their setup, kept stopped (you pay only for their disks) or running, and move them into service much faster than a fresh launch. In Kubernetes, low-priority placeholder pods reserve node space so real pods can schedule instantly while the cluster autoscaler adds nodes in the background. For known events, simply schedule capacity ahead of time.

Pitfall
Autoscaling moves the bottleneck
Going from 20 to 400 app instances, each with a 20-connection pool, means 8,000 database connections, far beyond what a PostgreSQL primary handles well. Put a pooler (PgBouncer, RDS Proxy) in between. Also check downstream rate limits, cache capacity, LB capacity and your cloud account's instance quotas before you trust the autoscaler at peak.
In the interview
What a strong answer includes
Name the metric and target (requests per instance at 70% of the load-tested max), the min and max bounds, scale-out-fast and scale-in-slow behaviour, and how you cover the startup delay (warm pool or scheduled pre-scaling). Then say what downstream system breaks first when the fleet grows tenfold.
