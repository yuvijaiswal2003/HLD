Design video conferencing (Zoom / Google Meet)

Video calls are the one design in this chapter where the main data path is not HTTP, not TCP and not a database: it is real-time media over UDP, where a packet that arrives 300 ms late is as useless as a lost one. The design separates three planes. The control plane (create meeting, join, permissions) is ordinary REST. The signaling plane exchanges session descriptions and network candidates over a WebSocket. The media plane carries encrypted audio and video between clients and media servers. Chapter 2 introduced WebRTC; this lesson builds a service on top of it.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: create a meeting and share a link; join from browser or app; 1:1 and group calls up to ~200 interactive participants.
  • Audio, video and screen share; mute, active speaker, gallery and speaker layouts; in-meeting chat (reuses Lesson 1).
  • Cloud recording; host controls such as waiting room and remove participant.
  • Latency: mouth-to-ear under ~150 ms one way, the ITU-T G.114 guideline for natural conversation; above ~400 ms people talk over each other.
  • Adapt to networks: keep audio intact and degrade video gracefully under packet loss or low bandwidth; one weak participant must not degrade everyone.
  • Connect from anywhere: behind NATs and corporate firewalls that block UDP.
  • Scale: ~10M concurrent participants at peak; media encrypted in transit (WebRTC mandates DTLS-SRTP).
In the interview
Name the three planes early
Ask about maximum meeting size, the 1:1 versus group mix, recording, dial-in and end-to-end encryption. Then say: 'I will separate control, signaling and media. Signaling is not part of WebRTC — we build it, usually on WebSockets. For media I will compare mesh, MCU and SFU with bandwidth numbers and pick an SFU with simulcast.' Interviewers listen for the topology trade-off and for NAT traversal with STUN and TURN.

5. High-level design

Join path: the client calls the meeting service, which checks permissions and returns a short-lived token, the signaling URL and ICE servers with temporary TURN credentials (an HMAC over an expiry time, so leaked credentials stop working quickly). The client opens the signaling WebSocket; signaling asks the allocator for an SFU in the meeting's region with spare capacity and records the assignment in Redis. Media path: the client exchanges an SDP offer and answer with the SFU through signaling, ICE finds a working network path, a DTLS handshake derives SRTP keys, and from then on audio and video flow directly between client and SFU. The SFU forwards each incoming stream to the other participants without decoding it.

6. Deep dives

Getting through NATs: ICE, STUN and TURN. Most devices sit behind NAT and do not know their public address. ICE (Interactive Connectivity Establishment) gathers candidate addresses and tests them in pairs: host candidates (local interfaces), server-reflexive candidates learned by asking a STUN server 'what IP and port do you see me as' (one cheap request and response, no media), and relay candidates allocated on a TURN server, which forwards all media when nothing else works — symmetric NATs, or firewalls that allow only TCP or TLS on port 443. STUN matters most for peer-to-peer 1:1 calls. With an SFU on a public IP running ICE-lite, the client can usually reach it directly and the SFU learns the client's public address from the connectivity checks themselves; TURN over TLS on 443, or ICE-TCP on the SFU, covers networks that block UDP. TURN costs real bandwidth, so deploy it in every region and use it only when needed.

Mesh (peer to peer)

Strengths
  • No media servers to run
  • Lowest latency on a direct path; end-to-end encryption is natural
Weaknesses
  • Each client encodes and uploads N-1 streams: 10 people need ~13.5 Mbps uplink
  • CPU and battery cost grow with every participant
Use when1:1 calls and groups of 3–4.

MCU (Multipoint Control Unit)

Strengths
  • Each client receives one composited stream: minimal downlink and decoding
  • Works for weak devices, phone dial-in and SIP room systems; easy recording
Weaknesses
  • Server decodes, mixes and re-encodes every stream: very CPU-heavy
  • Adds encoding latency, fixed layouts, quality loss; cannot work with end-to-end encryption
Use whenTelephony and legacy room-system bridges, composited recording or live-stream output.

SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit)

Strengths
  • Forwards packets without decoding: cheap CPU and little added latency
  • Each client uploads once; the server picks per-receiver quality, layouts are client-side
Weaknesses
  • Downlink grows with the number of visible streams
  • Large egress bandwidth bill; end-to-end encryption needs insertable streams
Use whenThe default for group calls from 3 to a few hundred participants.

Simulcast and bandwidth adaptation. With simulcast the sender encodes its camera at three qualities — say 720p at 1.5 Mbps, 360p at 500 kbps and 180p at 150 kbps — and sends all three to the SFU. The SFU then chooses per receiver and per stream: the active speaker's large tile gets the high layer, thumbnails get the low one, and a receiver on a 1 Mbps hotel network gets low layers for everyone, without dragging other participants down. The SFU estimates each receiver's downlink from congestion-control feedback (transport-wide congestion control and Google Congestion Control in WebRTC), switches layers at keyframes (requesting one with a PLI message), and tells senders to pause layers nobody is watching. Scalable Video Coding (SVC, in VP9 and AV1) achieves the same with one layered stream the SFU can thin out. For audio, the SFU forwards only the three or so loudest speakers, using the audio-level header extension (RFC 6464) so it never has to decode audio to know who is talking.

  • Allocating and cascading SFUs. The allocator places a meeting on an SFU in the region closest to its participants, with headroom for growth. Participants on other continents connect to their own nearest SFU, and the SFUs cascade: each stream crosses the backbone once per remote SFU, rather than once per remote participant over the public internet. Meetings too big for one server are split across several SFUs in a region in the same way.
  • Failure handling. SFUs heartbeat into the registry. When one dies, signaling sees its sessions drop, the allocator assigns a new SFU, and clients set up a fresh session with it (new SDP offer and answer, ICE and DTLS handshake; an ICE restart alone only covers a network change to the same SFU); users see a freeze of a few seconds. Nothing durable is lost because an SFU holds only in-flight packets, and room state lives in Redis so a signaling server can also fail over.
  • Quality under loss. Media uses UDP because TCP's retransmission and head-of-line blocking (Chapter 2) turn one lost packet into a stall. Receivers keep a small jitter buffer (tens of ms); video recovers lost packets with NACK-based retransmission when RTT is small, audio uses Opus in-band FEC, heavy loss triggers a keyframe request, and congestion control lowers bitrate before router queues build up.
  • Recording. A recording bot joins as a hidden participant and subscribes to every track. Writing each track separately is cheap and lets you composite offline in any layout; compositing live (MCU-style) is needed only to live-stream to a platform such as YouTube. Segments go to object storage with multipart upload, then a transcoding job produces playback renditions (Chapter 17).
  • Webinars with 10,000 viewers. Keep the few interactive speakers on the SFU and give viewers a composited HLS stream through a CDN, accepting several seconds of delay (low-latency HLS gets it down to a few seconds) in exchange for CDN-scale fan-out.
In production
Discord voice
Discord's 2018 engineering post on handling 2.5 million concurrent voice users with WebRTC describes clients connecting to Discord's own SFU, written in C++, rather than to each other, with voice servers deployed in many regions so users reach a nearby one.

PlacementNearest-region SFU, cascade across regionsAn extra backbone hop and more complex routing for global meetings
NAT traversalTURN in every region, short-lived credentialsRelay bandwidth is the most expensive traffic per user

Pitfall
Common video-call mistakes
Calling WebRTC 'peer-to-peer' and drawing a mesh for a 50-person call; forgetting that signaling is yours to build; sending media over TCP or WebSockets; leaving out TURN, so a slice of corporate users can never connect; and having the SFU transcode, which turns it into an MCU with MCU costs.
