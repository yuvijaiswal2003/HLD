Design Google Calendar

Design a calendar service like Google Calendar. Users create events, including recurring ones ('every weekday at 9:00', 'last Friday of the month'), invite attendees inside and outside the service, collect RSVPs, see day, week and month views across calendars they own or subscribe to, check colleagues' free/busy time, and receive reminders by push and email before events.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • Which recurrence patterns are required: full iCalendar RRULE, edits to single occurrences, 'this and following' edits?
  • Do we invite external attendees by email with iCalendar invitations and process their replies?
  • How precise must reminders be, and through which channels?
  • What is the sharing model: who sees event details and who sees only free/busy?
  • How large can events get, for example a 10,000-person all-hands?
  • Must we sync with third-party clients over CalDAV or similar protocols?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Do not store every occurrence of a recurring event forever. Store the rule plus exceptions, and decide when to expand it.
H2Store a recurring meeting's time as local wall-clock time plus an IANA time zone, not UTC. Work out why daylight saving time forces this.
H3A view asks 'what overlaps this window'. How do you index single events and also series that started years ago?
H4Reminders are delayed jobs (Chapter 9). Precompute only the next reminder per event, bucket by minute, and expect a spike just before every hour.
H5Invites: one canonical event owned by the organizer, plus a lightweight per-attendee entry for RSVP and personal settings.

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: create, edit and delete events with time zone, location, description and conferencing link; all-day events.
  • Recurrence with exceptions; invite internal and external attendees; RSVP yes, no or maybe.
  • Views across many calendars; share calendars with view or edit rights; free/busy lookup and find-a-time.
  • Reminders by push and email, configurable per event and per user.
  • Read-heavy: views are opened far more often than events change; view load under ~200 ms.
  • Timely reminders: within seconds of the scheduled time, and never twice.
  • Correct times across time zones and DST; no lost edits under concurrent changes.
  • Sync: changes reach every device and attendee within seconds.

4. Data model

Events live in sharded PostgreSQL, sharded by the organizer's calendar_id. Writes are modest (~7k/s at peak), and edits need transactions, versions (etags) and range queries. Each attendee's view is served from a per-calendar entries table and a materialized instances table on the attendee's own shard.

5. High-level design

Write path: the event service validates and writes the event, attendees and exceptions in one transaction on the organizer's shard, bumps the version and returns. A change event drives three consumers: the expander rewrites the affected occurrences in the instances table, the invite worker upserts attendee entries and sends invitations or updates, and the reminder planner schedules the next reminder per attendee. The organizer's own view expands the series inline from the event row, so they see the change immediately even if the expander lags. Read path: a week view is one range scan on instances by (calendar_id, start_utc) for each visible calendar, hydrated from a cache of event rows.

6. Deep dives

Recurrence: rule plus exceptions. Store the iCalendar RRULE (RFC 5545), for example FREQ=MONTHLY;BYDAY=-1FR for the last Friday of each month, never the full list of occurrences: an endless daily rule has no last occurrence. An edit to one occurrence becomes an exception row keyed by its original start time, which still identifies the occurrence after it is moved; Google's public Calendar API models this with recurringEventId and originalStartTime. 'This and following' splits the series: the old rule gets an UNTIL just before the split point and a new series starts there with the new rule. For queries, either expand rules on every read, which needs no storage but runs RRULE expansion in the read path, or materialize a rolling window. Materializing about 12 months ahead makes views and free/busy plain range scans; a nightly job extends the window, and the rare view beyond it expands on the fly.

Pitfall
The UTC trap
'Store everything in UTC' is right for log lines and wrong for recurring meetings. A weekly 09:00 New York meeting stored as 14:00 UTC would show at 10:00 local after clocks move forward in March. Time zone rules also change by government decision, so when the tz database updates, re-expand the instances in the affected zones.

Invites and RSVPs. The organizer's shard holds the one canonical event and the attendee list; each internal attendee gets a calendar_entries row on their own shard through an idempotent, asynchronous upsert, so their view stays a local query. Entries reference the event rather than copying it, so a title change is just a cache invalidation, not a fan-out — the 'fan out IDs, hydrate on read' rule from the news feed. An RSVP updates the attendee row on the organizer's shard and the attendee's entry. External attendees receive iMIP email (RFC 6047): a text/calendar part with METHOD:REQUEST, a stable UID and a SEQUENCE that increases with each significant change; their clients answer with METHOD:REPLY, which the inbound mail pipeline matches by UID. Concurrent edits use optimistic concurrency: the request carries the version it read (an etag), and a stale write gets 412 and retries on fresh data.

Reminders. This is the delayed-job scheduler from Chapter 9 with a sharp daily spike. The planner stores only the next reminder per (attendee, event), in a bucket for its fire minute, split into ~1,000 shards so many workers share the 08:50 burst. Workers lease shards (Chapter 8), prefetch buckets 2–5 minutes early and fire at the exact second. Edits do not chase down old reminder rows: at fire time the worker checks the stored version against the event and the attendee's status and drops stale reminders (lazy cancellation), while the planner writes a fresh row for the new time. Sending with an idempotency key of (user, event, occurrence, offset) means a worker that crashes and loses its lease cannot cause a double reminder. After an outage, reminders still before their event's start are sent late; the rest are dropped.

  • Free/busy and find-a-time. Range-scan each attendee's instances for the window, merge overlapping intervals and return only busy blocks for calendars whose details the requester may not see. Find-a-time intersects the free gaps inside working hours.
  • Big events. A 10,000-attendee all-hands means 10,000 entry upserts, done in batches by the invite worker; updates notify attendees in paced batches through the notification system.
  • Sync. Every change to a calendar appends to its change log, and a sync token encodes the last change_seq a client saw; clients send it to receive only what changed, and an expired token forces a full resync.

5Follow-up questions

  • The organizer moves a two-year weekly series 30 minutes later, but six occurrences already have individual edits. What happens to those exceptions?
  • A country abolishes daylight saving time with three months' notice. What does the system have to do?
  • How would you add meeting-room booking so a room can never be double-booked?
  • How would you send every user a daily agenda email at 07:00 in their own time zone?
  • How would you support a 'working location' or out-of-office that auto-declines new invites?
