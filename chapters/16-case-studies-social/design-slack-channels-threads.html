Design Slack-style channels with threads

Design the messaging core of a Slack-like workplace chat product. Workspaces contain public and private channels (some with 100,000+ members) and direct messages. Any message can start a thread of replies. Users see unread badges and mention counts for every channel on every device, and can search their workspace's history. Focus on channel delivery, threads, unread state and search.

1Clarify first

Write down what you would ask the interviewer before designing anything, then compare.

  • How large do workspaces and channels get: what is the biggest channel, and how many channels does a typical user belong to?
  • Is history kept forever, and do workspaces have retention or legal-hold policies?
  • How do threads behave: are replies shown in the channel, and who is notified about a reply?
  • Do unread badges need exact mention counts, consistent across devices in real time?
  • Must search respect private-channel membership, and how fresh must results be?
  • Are there enterprise needs such as data residency or customer-managed encryption keys?

2Your answer

3Hints 0/5

Stuck? Each hint gives a little more away than the last.

H1Reuse the chat lesson: gateways, per-conversation ordering, cursors. A channel is a conversation. What changes when it has 100k members?
H2Do not fan out per member in big channels. Let gateways subscribe to channels on behalf of their connected users, so each message travels once to each interested gateway.
H3A thread reply is a message with a parent reference. Keep it in the channel's partition, and keep a denormalized summary (reply count, last reply, participants) on the parent.
H4Unread state is a per-(user, channel) read cursor; mention counts need a per-user mentions index, and @channel in a huge channel should not write one row per member.
H5Search: index asynchronously from a change stream into an index routed by workspace, and filter by the channels the searcher may see at query time.

4Model solution

Try your own answer first. The solution follows seven steps: requirements, estimates, API, data model, architecture, deep dives, trade-offs.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: public and private channels, DMs; post, edit, delete; reactions; mentions (@user, @here, @channel).
  • Threads: reply to any message, optionally also post to the channel; a Threads view of followed threads.
  • Unread badge and mention count per channel and thread, consistent across devices.
  • Full-text search over everything the user may see; history retained per workspace policy.
  • Latency: under ~500 ms to online members, including in 100k-member channels.
  • Ordering: one order per channel, and inside each thread.
  • Durability: no acknowledged message is lost.
  • Isolation: tenants are isolated, and one huge workspace must not degrade others.
  • Freshness: search results within a few seconds.

4. Data model

A message is identified by (channel_id, ts), where ts is a server-assigned, per-channel, strictly increasing timestamp-like ID; a thread is identified by its parent's ts. Messages live in sharded MySQL behind Vitess, sharded by channel_id: every hot query is per channel, and a reply plus its parent's summary update can then commit in one shard-local transaction.

5. High-level design

Write path: the API dedupes on client_msg_id, assigns the channel's next ts, commits to the channel's shard and publishes the message to the channel server that owns the channel. Delivery: when a user connects, their gateway subscribes to each of that user's channels (reference-counted across all its users); the channel server keeps the set of subscribed gateways per channel and sends one frame to each, and gateways fan out to their local sockets. Side effects: a change stream drives mention and unread updates, search indexing and push or email notifications for offline or mentioned users. Read path: history is a range scan on (channel_id, ts) in one shard; on startup the client fetches its channel list with latest_ts and last_read_ts to draw all badges in one call.

In production
How Slack describes it
Slack's engineering blog describes a real-time layer of stateful channel servers, each owning a set of channels via consistent hashing, and gateway servers that hold user WebSockets and subscribe to channels on behalf of connected users. Separately, Slack wrote about moving from MySQL shards keyed by workspace, where the largest customers created hot shards, to Vitess, which let data such as messages be sharded by channel.

6. Deep dives

Threads. A thread is not a separate conversation. Replies live in the parent's channel partition with thread_ts set, so they share the channel's ordering and shard, and the thread index lists them in order. The parent carries a denormalized summary (reply_count, latest_reply_ts, the first few reply_users) updated in the same transaction as the reply, so rendering a channel never counts replies. Followers are the parent's author, everyone who replied or was mentioned in the thread, and anyone who clicked Follow. Every channel subscriber gets a small 'thread updated' event to refresh the summary; followers additionally get unread state in their Threads view and, if offline or mentioned, a notification. 'Also send to channel' writes a reference into the main timeline at the reply's ts.

Unread badges and mention counts. A channel is unread for a user when latest_ts exceeds that user's last_read_ts, a comparison the client can make for 150 channels from one multi-get. Mention counts come from the per-user mentions table: direct mentions after last_read_ts. For @channel or @here in a 100k-member channel, writing 100k mention rows would be the celebrity problem again, so store the broadcast once on the channel and add it at read time for every member whose cursor is behind it — the same push-versus-pull hybrid as the news feed. Marking a channel read on the laptop moves the cursor and emits a channel_marked event to the user's other devices, clearing the badge everywhere.

Search with permissions. A change stream from the message shards feeds an indexer (Chapter 10); Elasticsearch refreshes about once a second, so new messages are searchable within seconds, and edits and deletes flow through the same stream. Small workspaces share indices with routing by workspace_id so a query hits one shard; the largest tenants get dedicated indices. Every query carries a filter: public channels of the workspace OR channel_id in the searcher's private channels and DMs. Even thousands of IDs fit in an Elasticsearch terms filter (the default limit is 65,536 terms), and because the filter is evaluated at query time, removing someone from a private channel takes effect immediately.

  • Huge channels. Delivery cost is bounded by the gateway count, not member count. Notification fan-out for @channel goes through the notification system with pacing, and product guardrails (a warning or admin restriction on @channel above a size) keep 100k pushes rare.
  • Hot workspaces and channels. Sharding by channel spreads a 200k-user workspace across many shards; a single very hot channel sits on one channel server and one shard, which handle thousands of messages a second, far above human posting rates.
  • Reconnect storms. A user reconnecting re-subscribes to ~150 channels; gateways batch subscriptions per channel server, and clients reconnect with jittered backoff.

5Follow-up questions

  • A 200k-member announcement channel posts and every client fetches it at once. What breaks first, and how do you protect it?
  • How do edits and deletes propagate so that every device, thread summaries and the search index converge?
  • An enterprise customer requires EU data residency and its own encryption keys. What changes?
  • How would you build a channel shared between two different companies' workspaces?
  • A user reads a channel on the phone while the laptop is offline. How do badges end up correct on both?
