Design a notification system

Almost every product needs to reach users outside the app: one-time passwords (OTPs), 'your order shipped', 'Alice mentioned you', marketing campaigns. A notification system is a shared platform that other teams call; it decides how to deliver a message (channel, template, timing, retries), not whether the business event happened. Chat (Lesson 1) and the feed (Lesson 2) both use it for anyone who is offline. Its core is queues and delivery semantics from Chapter 9 and resilience patterns from Chapter 12.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: send through push (APNs for iOS, FCM for Android and web), SMS, email and an in-app inbox.
  • Triggered by internal services (transactional: OTP, receipts, mentions) and by a campaign tool (bulk: millions of users per send).
  • Templates per channel and language; user preferences per category and channel; quiet hours in the user's time zone.
  • Scheduled sends; delivery tracking (sent, delivered, opened, clicked, failed); device token registration.
  • Latency by priority: OTP and security alerts in a few seconds; marketing may take tens of minutes.
  • No loss: an accepted notification is eventually sent or explicitly marked failed (at-least-once).
  • No spam: deduplicate retries, enforce per-user frequency caps, honor opt-outs (also a legal requirement for marketing email and SMS).
  • Isolation: a 100M-user campaign must not delay a password-reset code; a provider outage must not stall other channels.
In the interview
Clarify the shape of the traffic
Ask which channels, transactional versus marketing split, latency per class, whether the platform owns user preferences, and whether exactly-once is expected. Then say early that exactly-once to an external provider is not achievable — a worker can crash after the provider accepted the message but before recording it — so the goal is at-least-once with deduplication, which makes duplicates rare rather than impossible.

5. High-level design

The API validates the request, rejects a repeated idempotency key, writes to the intake topic and returns 202 Accepted; callers never wait on a provider. Router workers expand the request: look up the user's preferences and devices, drop channels the user opted out of, defer the send to the end of quiet hours (through the delayed-job scheduler from Chapter 9) unless it is critical, check frequency caps, render the template in the user's language, and emit one job per device or address to the right channel and priority topic. Channel workers call the provider and record the result; provider callbacks (SES events, Twilio status webhooks) update the log later.

6. Deep dives

Deduplication without exactly-once. Duplicates come from two places: a caller retrying its HTTP request, and Kafka redelivering after a worker crash. The API handles the first with the idempotency key (SET idem:key NX EX 86400). The worker handles the second with a per-(notification, device) key: it claims the key with a short lease before calling the provider and marks it sent afterwards. A redelivered job that finds sent skips; one that finds an expired claim sends again. The remaining window — provider accepted, worker died before marking — is why the result is 'almost never twice', not 'exactly once'. Kafka transactions do not help here: they cover Kafka-to-Kafka processing, not an HTTP call to Apple. For push, also set apns-collapse-id or an FCM collapse_key so a duplicate replaces the earlier notification on the device instead of stacking.

  • Priority isolation. Separate topics and worker pools per channel and per priority — bulkheads. OTPs flow through sms-high, which is never shared with marketing. A campaign planner does not dump 100M jobs at once: it expands the segment in batches of ~10k, releases them through a token bucket sized to the provider quota, and with local_time spreads sends across time zones.
  • Preferences and quiet hours. Categories carry a policy: security and transactional messages cannot be turned off, marketing honors opt-out on every channel. Preferences are read at send time (from cache, invalidated on write) so an unsubscribe takes effect within seconds, not at the next campaign build.
  • Frequency caps and aggregation. Per-user caps such as 'at most 3 marketing pushes a day' are Redis counters keyed by user, category and day (Chapter 11 covers the window algorithms). Social notifications are aggregated: hold likes for a post in a 1–5 minute window keyed by (user, category, object) and send 'Alice and 12 others liked your photo' once.
  • Templates. Versioned per channel and locale, rendered by the router, parameters validated at the API so a bad request fails fast. Store the rendered content or template version in the log so support can see exactly what a user received.
  • Tracking. Providers report delivery asynchronously: SES publishes delivery and bounce events, Twilio calls a status webhook. APNs and FCM confirm acceptance, not display, so the app reports 'received' and 'opened' itself. Email opens via tracking pixels are unreliable since Apple Mail Privacy Protection preloads images; click-through via redirect links is the more trustworthy signal.

Log storeCassandra with TTLNo ad-hoc analytics; stream status events to the warehouse (Chapter 10)
Pitfall
What weak answers miss
Calling providers synchronously inside the caller's request; one shared queue for OTPs and campaigns; retrying permanent failures forever (wasted capacity, and repeated hard bounces damage email sender reputation — Amazon SES can pause an account whose bounce rate stays high); ignoring time zones for quiet hours and campaigns; and claiming exactly-once delivery to an external provider.
