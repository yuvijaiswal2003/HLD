Design a news feed (Twitter / Instagram)

The home feed is the canonical read-heavy fan-out problem: one write (a post) must appear in the feeds of thousands or millions of followers, and feeds are read far more often than posts are written. This walkthrough designs a Twitter/Instagram-style home timeline. Chapter 7 introduced the celebrity hot spot and Chapter 14 previewed fan-out strategies; here the decision is made with numbers.

1. Requirements

  • Functional: create a post (short text, optional images or video stored in object storage behind a CDN, Chapters 5 and 10).
  • Follow and unfollow; the graph is asymmetric (following needs no approval).
  • Home feed of posts from followed accounts, ranked, with a chronological option and infinite scroll.
  • Profile timeline of one user's own posts; like and reply counts on every post.
  • Fast reads: first feed page under ~200 ms p99 server-side.
  • Read-heavy: roughly 100 feed reads per post written.
  • Freshness: followers may see a post a few seconds late (eventual consistency), but the author must see it immediately (read-your-writes, Chapter 7).
  • Availability: a slightly stale feed beats an error page.
In the interview
The three questions that flip the design
(1) What does the follower distribution look like — is there a long tail of accounts with tens of millions of followers? (2) Ranked or chronological? (3) How deep does the feed go? Users rarely scroll past a few hundred items, which caps how much you precompute. Then say out loud that you will compare fan-out on write with fan-out on read and land on a hybrid; interviewers are waiting for that trade-off.

5. High-level design

Write path: the post service stores the post, appends its ID to the author's timeline and emits post_created. Fan-out workers page through the author's followers in batches of a few thousand and push the post ID onto each active follower's home timeline, trimming to ~800 entries. Only 8-byte IDs are fanned out, never post bodies, so an edit never needs a second fan-out. Read path: the feed service reads the user's precomputed ID list, merges recent posts from any celebrity accounts they follow, hydrates the IDs with a multi-get against the post cache (plus counts and author profiles), ranks, and returns 20 items with a cursor. The author's own latest posts are merged from their author timeline, so they always see their post instantly even if fan-out lags.

6. Deep dives

Fan-out on write (push)

Strengths
  • A feed read is one cache lookup: fast and predictable
  • Work is done once per post, off the request path
Weaknesses
  • Write amplification: one post becomes follower-count inserts
  • Celebrity posts land slowly and hammer the cache
  • Wasted work for followers who never open the app
Use whenOrdinary accounts below a follower threshold in a read-heavy product.

Fan-out on read (pull)

Strengths
  • Writes are trivial: append to the author's timeline
  • No wasted work for inactive users; always fresh
Weaknesses
  • Every read fans in across hundreds of followees
  • High, variable read latency; harder to rank and paginate
Use whenCelebrity authors, returning inactive users, or small systems.

Hybrid

Strengths
  • Push for normal authors, pull only for the few celebrities a reader follows
  • Bounds both write amplification and read fan-in
Weaknesses
  • Two code paths to build, test and keep consistent
  • Threshold tuning, and accounts that cross it
Use whenThe standard answer at Twitter or Instagram scale.

The celebrity hybrid in detail. Mark accounts above a follower threshold (say 100k to 1M, tuned from fan-out lag metrics) as pull authors; their posts go only to their author timeline. At read time the feed service looks up which pull authors the reader follows — usually a few dozen at most, cached per user — reads their newest post IDs in one pipelined Redis round trip, and merges them with the precomputed list by post ID, which sorts by time because it is a Snowflake. Following 30 celebrities costs ~30 cheap reads per feed load instead of 50M writes per celebrity post. The same idea covers inactive users from the other side: skip fan-out to anyone who has not opened the app in ~30 days, and rebuild their timeline by pulling when they return. Rebuilds also cover a lost Redis node, because the home timeline is a cache, not the source of truth.

Ranking. A ranked feed is a two-stage pipeline. Candidate generation gathers a few hundred post IDs: the precomputed timeline, the celebrity merge and optionally recommended posts from accounts the user does not follow. Scoring hydrates features — affinity with the author, post age, engagement so far, media type — and a model predicts the probability of engagement; a heuristic such as affinity x engagement / (age_hours + 2)^1.5 is a fine interview stand-in. A final pass applies rules: dedupe, author diversity (not five posts in a row from one account) and freshness. Rank when the feed is opened, not at fan-out time, because scores decay with age and depend on the reader.

Pagination. Offset pagination breaks on a live feed: new posts push everything down, so page 2 repeats items from page 1 (Chapter 11). For a chronological feed, use a keyset cursor — 'items with post_id below the last one I saw' — which is stable because Snowflake IDs sort by time. For a ranked feed, scores move between requests, so rank once per session, cache the ordered ID list (a few KB) for 10–30 minutes, and make the cursor an opaque token holding the session ID and position. Pull-to-refresh starts a new session.

  • Hot posts and counters. A viral post is hydrated millions of times a minute: replicate the hot key or keep a small in-process cache on feed servers (Chapter 5). Likes go to sharded counters (N sub-keys per post, summed on read) flushed to the database in batches; a count a few seconds stale is fine.
  • Deletes, blocks and privacy. Never chase a deleted post through millions of timelines. Hydration drops IDs whose post no longer exists and the read path filters blocked or muted authors, so correctness lives on the read side.
  • Follow and unfollow. On follow, asynchronously backfill the newest ~20 posts of the new followee; on unfollow, filter at read time and let stale IDs age out of the capped list.
  • Fan-out throughput. Key the Kafka topic by author_id so one author's posts fan out in order, and batch Redis writes with pipelines: at ~115k inserts/s average, about a dozen workers each sustaining ~10k pipelined writes/s cover the average, and autoscaling on consumer lag covers peaks.
In production
Twitter's timelines
Twitter's 2012 'Timelines at Scale' talk described this architecture: tweets fanned out on write into Redis-backed home timelines capped at roughly 800 entries, with accounts that have tens of millions of followers called out as the hard case for fan-out latency.

7. Trade-offs and bottlenecks

Bottleneck / riskMitigation
Celebrity write amplificationHybrid: pull authors merged at read time; threshold tuned from fan-out lag
Redis memory for timelinesStore IDs only, cap at ~800, skip inactive users, rebuild on demand
Hydration latency (20+ lookups per page)Multi-get against the post cache, cached author profiles, hot-key replication
Fan-out lag during spikes (big news events)Kafka absorbs bursts; workers autoscale on consumer lag; authors see their own posts via read-time merge
Ranking costScore a few hundred candidates, not thousands; cache the ranked list per session
Deleted or blocked content in timelinesFilter at read time; never depend on timeline cleanup
Pitfall
Two classic mistakes
Choosing pure fan-out on write and hand-waving the celebrity case, and storing full post bodies in every follower's timeline — that multiplies storage by follower count and turns every edit into a fan-out job. Fan out IDs; hydrate on read.
